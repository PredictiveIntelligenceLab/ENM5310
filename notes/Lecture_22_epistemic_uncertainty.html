This section of the course. The next three lectures return to uncertainty. Today covers epistemic uncertainty — what the model does not know because it has seen finite data — for deep networks. The following two cover aleatoric uncertainty — irreducible randomness in the data, which for high-dimensional outputs means learning a full distribution — through generative models: VAEs (Lecture 23), then diffusion and flow matching (Lecture 24). Two tools introduced today come back in both: the evidence lower bound and the reparameterization trick (§7), and Langevin dynamics (§8).

The point of this lecture. Since Lecture 12, every network we trained returned a single parameter vector and a single prediction. Lecture 7 showed what a Bayesian treatment buys for linear models: a predictive distribution whose epistemic part grows away from the data. For networks with millions of parameters the exact posterior is out of reach. This lecture goes from practice to principle. First the methods people actually use: a Bayesian last layer, MC dropout, deep ensembles, SWAG, and two cheap additions to ensembles (randomized priors and epinets). Then the principled machinery that explains and extends them: variational inference and stochastic-gradient MCMC. One question organizes all of it: what distribution over functions does each method produce, and at what cost?

A note on notation: in this lecture qqq denotes an approximating distribution over parameters, as in Murphy. The empirical distribution of the data, written q(x)q(x)q(x) elsewhere in the course, does not appear; data averages are written as explicit sums.

0. Why epistemic uncertainty, and how to measure it (8 min)

0.1 Two sources of uncertainty

Lecture 7 split predictive uncertainty into two parts. In general, if a model's parameters are uncertain, the law of total variance gives, for a scalar output,

Var⁡[y∣x,D]=Eθ∣D[Var⁡(y∣x,θ)]⏟aleatoric+Var⁡θ∣D[E(y∣x,θ)]⏟epistemic. \operatorname{Var}[y\mid x,\mathcal D] = \underbrace{\mathbb E_{\theta\mid\mathcal D}\big[\operatorname{Var}(y\mid x,\theta)\big]}_{\text{aleatoric}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_{\theta\mid\mathcal D}\big[\mathbb E(y\mid x,\theta)\big]}_{\text{epistemic}} . Var[y∣x,D]=aleatoricEθ∣D​[Var(y∣x,θ)]​​+epistemicVarθ∣D​[E(y∣x,θ)]​​.
  • Aleatoric uncertainty is noise in the data-generating process: sensor noise, turbulent fluctuations, unresolved scales. More data from the same process does not reduce it.
  • Epistemic uncertainty is disagreement among models that all fit the data. It is large where data are sparse and shrinks as data accumulate.

For classification the analogous split uses entropy: the total predictive entropy H[Eθ p(y∣x,θ)]\mathbb H[\mathbb E_\theta\,p(y\mid x,\theta)]H[Eθ​p(y∣x,θ)] equals the expected entropy Eθ H[p(y∣x,θ)]\mathbb E_\theta\,\mathbb H[p(y\mid x,\theta)]Eθ​H[p(y∣x,θ)] (aleatoric) plus the mutual information between yyy and θ\thetaθ (epistemic).

0.2 Why it matters in science and engineering

  • Active learning and Bayesian optimization (Lecture 11): the next experiment or simulation should go where the model is ignorant, not where the data are noisy. Only the epistemic term carries that information.
  • Out-of-distribution detection: a neural operator queried far outside its training distribution should say so.
  • Trusting a surrogate: replacing a solver with a network is defensible only if you know where the network is reliable.

0.3 The problem with a single network

A network trained by MLE or MAP is a point estimate (Lecture 12 §3.4). Its predictive distribution contains only the aleatoric noise model. Far from the data, a ReLU network extrapolates linearly with whatever confidence its outputs imply. The goal of this lecture is to put the epistemic term back.

A running example. Throughout, picture 1D regression with data on [−1,−0.3]∪[0.3,1][-1,-0.3]\cup[0.3,1][−1,−0.3]∪[0.3,1] and a gap in between. A good method should be confident where there is data, more uncertain in the gap ("in-between" uncertainty), and increasingly uncertain outside [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1]. A Gaussian process (Lecture 8) does all three. Several popular approximations do not (Foong et al., 2020).

1. The Bayesian ideal for neural networks, and why it is hard (8 min)

1.1 The ideal

Exactly as for Bayesian linear regression:

  1. Model: a prior p(θ)p(\theta)p(θ), typically N(0,α2I)\mathcal N(0,\alpha^2I)N(0,α2I), and a likelihood p(y∣x,θ)p(y\mid x,\theta)p(y∣x,θ) built from the network.
  2. Inference: the posterior p(θ∣D)∝p(D∣θ) p(θ)p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\propto p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)p(θ∣D)∝p(D∣θ)p(θ).
  3. Prediction: the posterior predictive, a Bayesian model average:
p(y∣x,D)=∫p(y∣x,θ) p(θ∣D) dθ  ≈  1M∑m=1Mp(y∣x,θm),θm∼p(θ∣D). p(y\mid x,\mathcal D) = \int p(y\mid x,\theta)\,p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\,d\theta\;\approx\;\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}p(y\mid x,\theta_m),\qquad \theta_m\sim p(\theta\mid\mathcal D). p(y∣x,D)=∫p(y∣x,θ)p(θ∣D)dθ≈M1​m=1∑M​p(y∣x,θm​),θm​∼p(θ∣D).

Every method in this lecture produces a set of plausible networks {θm}\{\theta_m\}{θm​}, or a distribution to sample them from, and averages their predictions.

1.2 Why it is hard

  • Dimension. PPP is in the millions. No grid, no exact normalization, and anything requiring a P×PP\times PP×P covariance is out of the question.
  • No conjugacy. The network is nonlinear in θ\thetaθ, so the posterior is not Gaussian or any other closed form.
  • Multimodality. The posterior has many separated modes. Some are trivially equivalent — permuting hidden units gives the same function (Lecture 12 §3.3) — but many correspond to genuinely different functions that fit the data equally well.
  • Priors on weights are opaque. A Gaussian on weights induces some distribution over functions — for infinitely wide networks, a GP (Lecture 12 §6) — but it is hard to reason about what a weight prior means for a finite deep network.

A useful shift in viewpoint. We do not care about θ\thetaθ itself; we care about the distribution over functions it induces, and in particular about the spread of f(x;θ)f(x;\theta)f(x;θ) at the inputs we will query. Two very different weight vectors that compute the same function are, for prediction, the same sample. Keep this in mind when judging each method: does it produce functionally diverse networks?

2. A Bayesian last layer (8 min)

The cheapest way to recover a posterior is to be Bayesian about only the part of the network where Bayes is easy. Write the network as

f(x;θ)=w⊤ϕ(x), f(x;\theta)=w^\top\phi(x), f(x;θ)=w⊤ϕ(x),

with ϕ\phiϕ the penultimate features (Lecture 12 §3.1). Train the network as usual, freeze ϕ\phiϕ, and put a Gaussian prior w∼N(0,α2I)w\sim\mathcal N(0,\alpha^2I)w∼N(0,α2I) on the last layer. For a Gaussian likelihood the posterior over www is then exactly Gaussian, and the predictive is Lecture 7's formula with learned features:

p(y∣x,D)=N(μw⊤ϕ(x), ϕ(x)⊤Σw ϕ(x)⏟epistemic+σ2),Σw=(1σ2Φ⊤Φ+α−2I)−1,μw=1σ2ΣwΦ⊤y, p(y\mid x,\mathcal D) = \mathcal N\Big(\mu_w^\top\phi(x),\ \underbrace{\phi(x)^\top\Sigma_w\,\phi(x)}_{\text{epistemic}}+\sigma^2\Big),\qquad \Sigma_w = \Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Phi^\top\Phi+\alpha^{-2}I\Big)^{-1},\quad \mu_w=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma_w\Phi^\top y, p(y∣x,D)=N(μw⊤​ϕ(x), epistemicϕ(x)⊤Σw​ϕ(x)​​+σ2),Σw​=(σ21​Φ⊤Φ+α−2I)−1,μw​=σ21​Σw​Φ⊤y,

where Φ\PhiΦ stacks ϕ(xn)⊤\phi(x_n)^\topϕ(xn​)⊤ as rows. For non-Gaussian likelihoods (classification), the last-layer posterior is not Gaussian, and the standard fix is a Gaussian approximation at its mode (a last-layer Laplace approximation; Daxberger et al., 2021).

Deep learning learns the features; Bayesian linear regression supplies the uncertainty. It costs one training run plus a d×dd\times dd×d matrix inverse, it is post hoc, and it is a strong baseline.

When it fails. All the epistemic uncertainty lives in feature space. If the learned features ϕ(x)\phi(x)ϕ(x) collapse far from the data — mapping unfamiliar inputs onto familiar feature vectors — then ϕ(x)⊤Σwϕ(x)\phi(x)^\top\Sigma_w\phi(x)ϕ(x)⊤Σw​ϕ(x) stays small and the model cannot know it is extrapolating. In the running example, a ReLU network's features often vary little in the gap, and the last layer reports confidence there. Methods that vary the features as well as the readout avoid this; that is the rest of the lecture.

Remember. Freeze the trained features and do Bayesian linear regression on the last layer: exact for Gaussian likelihoods, one training run, epistemic variance ϕ(x)⊤Σwϕ(x)\phi(x)^\top\Sigma_w\phi(x)ϕ(x)⊤Σw​ϕ(x). It is only as good as the features: if they collapse away from the data, so does the uncertainty.

3. MC dropout (5 min)

Lecture 13 used dropout as a regularizer and switched it off at test time. MC dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) leaves it on: run MMM stochastic forward passes, each with a fresh dropout mask, and treat them as MMM networks:

p(y∣x,D)≈1M∑m=1Mp(y∣x,θ⊙maskm), p(y\mid x,\mathcal D)\approx\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}p\big(y\mid x,\theta\odot\text{mask}_m\big), p(y∣x,D)≈M1​m=1∑M​p(y∣x,θ⊙maskm​),

with the epistemic variance given by the spread of the MMM predicted means.

It is attractive because it is free: any network trained with dropout already supports it, and the only cost is MMM forward passes. Its weakness is that the amount of uncertainty is set by the dropout rate, a hyperparameter chosen for regularization, not inferred from data. In particular the spread does not shrink in the right way as data accumulate, as a posterior's should (Osband, 2016), and on the running example it tends to miss the in-between uncertainty (Foong et al., 2020). §7 shows it can be read as variational inference with a particular family, which explains both its appeal and these limits. Treat it as a cheap baseline, not a posterior.

4. Deep ensembles (10 min)

4.1 The method

Train MMM networks independently, from different random initializations and data orders, and average their predictions (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). For regression, let each network output a mean and a variance, (μm(x),σm2(x))\big(\mu_m(x),\sigma_m^2(x)\big)(μm​(x),σm2​(x)), trained by the Gaussian negative log-likelihood. The ensemble predictive is the mixture, with moments

μ∗(x)=1M∑mμm(x),σ∗2(x)=1M∑mσm2(x)⏟aleatoric+1M∑m(μm(x)−μ∗(x))2⏟epistemic, \mu_*(x) = \frac1M\sum_m\mu_m(x),\qquad \sigma_*^2(x) = \underbrace{\frac1M\sum_m\sigma_m^2(x)}_{\text{aleatoric}}+\underbrace{\frac1M\sum_m\big(\mu_m(x)-\mu_*(x)\big)^2}_{\text{epistemic}}, μ∗​(x)=M1​m∑​μm​(x),σ∗2​(x)=aleatoricM1​m∑​σm2​(x)​​+epistemicM1​m∑​(μm​(x)−μ∗​(x))2​​,

the law of total variance of §0.1, with ensemble members as posterior samples.

4.2 Why it works

It was first proposed as a non-Bayesian method, but the best explanation is Bayesian. Different random initializations end in different modes of the loss landscape, and those modes compute genuinely different functions away from the data (Fort et al., 2019). Methods that explore around a single mode — the last layer of §2, SWAG in §5, the variational methods of §7 — capture variation within one basin; ensembles capture variation between basins, which is where most functional diversity lives. An ensemble is therefore a coarse but well-placed approximation to the Bayesian model average (Wilson & Izmailov, 2020). When Izmailov et al. (2021) computed a reference posterior for image classifiers with full-batch Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (§8), deep ensembles came closer to it than the standard approximate-inference methods.

Empirically, deep ensembles are among the most reliable methods under dataset shift (Ovadia et al., 2019), and they are the baseline every new method must beat. M=5M=5M=5 already captures much of the benefit.

4.3 Limitations

  • Cost: MMM times the training and inference.
  • Collapse where members agree for the wrong reason. If all members share the same inductive bias, they can make the same confident extrapolation far from the data; nothing in training forces them to disagree there. §6 addresses this.
  • No explicit prior. Uncertainty in unexplored regions depends on accidents of initialization rather than on a stated prior.

Remember. Train MMM networks from different initializations and average. Mixture variance = mean of member variances (aleatoric) + spread of member means (epistemic). They work because different initializations reach functionally different modes. Simple, strong, M×M\timesM× cost.

5. SWAG (6 min)

SWAG — stochastic weight averaging Gaussian (Maddox et al., 2019) — builds a posterior approximation from a single training run, using the SGD trajectory itself.

  1. Train normally.
  2. Continue SGD with a constant or cyclical, relatively high learning rate, so the iterates bounce around the basin. Record KKK snapshots θ1,…,θK\theta_1,\dots,\theta_Kθ1​,…,θK​, typically once per epoch.
  3. Fit a Gaussian N(θˉ,Σ)\mathcal N(\bar\theta,\Sigma)N(θˉ,Σ) to the snapshots: mean θˉ=1K∑kθk\bar\theta=\frac1K\sum_k\theta_kθˉ=K1​∑k​θk​; covariance as the sum of a diagonal part and a rank-RRR part built from the last RRR deviations D=[θk−θˉ]D=[\theta_k-\bar\theta]D=[θk​−θˉ]:
Σ=12(Σdiag+1R−1DD⊤). \Sigma = \tfrac12\Big(\Sigma_{\text{diag}}+\tfrac{1}{R-1}DD^\top\Big). Σ=21​(Σdiag​+R−11​DD⊤).
  1. At test time, sample θm∼N(θˉ,Σ)\theta_m\sim\mathcal N(\bar\theta,\Sigma)θm​∼N(θˉ,Σ), recompute batch-normalization statistics if used, and average predictions.

Storage is about (R+2)P(R+2)P(R+2)P numbers, with R≈20R\approx20R≈20. The rationale: SGD with a constant step size behaves like a noisy sampler whose spread reflects the local geometry of the loss — an idea made precise by SGLD in §8. SWAG covers one basin, so it captures within-mode diversity only. MultiSWAG, an ensemble of SWAG models, combines within-basin and between-basin diversity and often beats both components (Wilson & Izmailov, 2020).

6. Cheap additions to ensembles: randomized priors and epinets (7 min)

Two auxiliary ideas address the ensemble's weaknesses in §4.3: no explicit prior, and the M×M\timesM× cost.

Randomized prior functions (Osband et al., 2018). Give each ensemble member a fixed, randomly initialized prior network pmp_mpm​ that is never trained:

fm(x)=gθm(x)+β pm(x). f_m(x) = g_{\theta_m}(x)+\beta\,p_m(x). fm​(x)=gθm​​(x)+βpm​(x).

Train gθmg_{\theta_m}gθm​​ as usual. Near the data, gθmg_{\theta_m}gθm​​ learns to cancel the prior function so that fmf_mfm​ fits the data. Far from the data, nothing forces it to, and fmf_mfm​ reverts toward its own random prior function. The members therefore disagree by construction where there is no data, and β\betaβ sets how much — an explicit knob for prior uncertainty, at no extra cost. The idea has an exact justification: for Bayesian linear regression, solving the regularized least-squares problem with perturbed targets and a regularizer anchored at a random prior draw, 1σ2∥y+ε~−Φθ∥2+1τ2∥θ−θ0∥2\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\lVert y+\tilde\varepsilon-\Phi\theta\rVert^2+\frac{1}{\tau^2}\lVert\theta-\theta_0\rVert^2σ21​∥y+ε~−Φθ∥2+τ21​∥θ−θ0​∥2, returns an exact posterior sample (practice problem P4; Code Checkpoint 22B). The prior network is θ0\theta_0θ0​ expressed in function space.

Epinets (Osband et al., 2023). An ensemble indexes its beliefs by a member number m∈{1,…,M}m\in\{1,\dots,M\}m∈{1,…,M}. Generalize: an epistemic neural network fθ(x,z)f_\theta(x,z)fθ​(x,z) takes an extra epistemic index zzz, and varying zzz with xxx fixed generates the model's range of beliefs. An epinet implements this cheaply by adding a small network, with a learnable part and a fixed randomized-prior part, on top of the frozen features of a large base model, with z∼N(0,I)z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)z∼N(0,I). Hundreds of index samples cost little more than one forward pass of the base network, and on image classification epinets match large ensembles at a fraction of the cost.

Why joint predictions matter. Osband et al. also argue that epistemic uncertainty should be judged on joint predictions over several inputs. A fair coin and a coin of unknown uniform bias both give P(heads)=12P(\text{heads})=\tfrac12P(heads)=21​, but two heads in a row have probability 14\tfrac1441​ and E[p2]=13\mathbb E[p^2]=\tfrac13E[p2]=31​ respectively. Epistemic uncertainty makes outcomes correlated: one observation is informative about the next. An active-learning or BO loop (Lecture 11) relies on exactly this, so draw one network (or one zzz) and use it for all candidate points.

Remember. Randomized priors: add a fixed random network to each ensemble member so members disagree away from the data; exact for linear models. Epinets: index an ensemble with a continuous zzz in a small add-on network. Judge epistemic uncertainty by joint predictions, not only marginal ones.

7. Variational inference (16 min)

The methods so far are practical, but none comes with a statement of what posterior it approximates. Variational inference supplies one.

7.1 Inference as optimization

Choose a tractable family of distributions qψ(θ)q_\psi(\theta)qψ​(θ) and find the member closest to the posterior:

ψ⋆=arg⁡min⁡ψ KL(qψ(θ) ∥ p(θ∣D)). \psi^\star = \arg\min_\psi\ \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\psi(\theta)\,\big\|\,p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\big). ψ⋆=argψmin​ KL(qψ​(θ)∥∥​p(θ∣D)).

This KL involves the unknown normalizer p(D)p(\mathcal D)p(D), so it cannot be computed directly. The way around it is the central identity of this section and of the next lecture.

7.2 The evidence lower bound

  1. Write the KL and use Bayes' rule, p(θ∣D)=p(D∣θ)p(θ)/p(D)p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)=p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)p(\theta)/p(\mathcal D)p(θ∣D)=p(D∣θ)p(θ)/p(D):
KL(qψ ∥ p(⋅∣D))=Eqψ[log⁡qψ(θ)−log⁡p(D∣θ)−log⁡p(θ)]+log⁡p(D). \mathrm{KL}(q_\psi\,\|\,p(\cdot\mid\mathcal D)) = \mathbb E_{q_\psi}\big[\log q_\psi(\theta)-\log p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)-\log p(\theta)\big]+\log p(\mathcal D). KL(qψ​∥p(⋅∣D))=Eqψ​​[logqψ​(θ)−logp(D∣θ)−logp(θ)]+logp(D).
  1. Rearrange:
log⁡p(D)=Eqψ[log⁡p(D∣θ)]−KL(qψ(θ) ∥ p(θ))⏟ELBO(ψ)+KL(qψ ∥ p(⋅∣D)). \log p(\mathcal D) = \underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\psi}\big[\log p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)\big]-\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\psi(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta)\big)}_{\text{ELBO}(\psi)}+\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\psi\,\|\,p(\cdot\mid\mathcal D)\big). logp(D)=ELBO(ψ)Eqψ​​[logp(D∣θ)]−KL(qψ​(θ)∥p(θ))​​+KL(qψ​∥p(⋅∣D)).
  1. The last KL is non-negative, so the ELBO is a lower bound on the log evidence, and maximizing the ELBO over ψ\psiψ is the same as minimizing the KL to the posterior — because log⁡p(D)\log p(\mathcal D)logp(D) does not depend on ψ\psiψ.

The two terms have clear roles. The expected log-likelihood rewards fitting the data; the KL to the prior penalizes distributions that are more concentrated than the data justify. With qψq_\psiqψ​ a point mass, the ELBO reduces (up to a constant) to the MAP objective: VI is MAP with a distribution instead of a point.

7.3 Mean-field Gaussian VI and the reparameterization trick

The standard choice for networks is a fully factorized Gaussian, qψ(θ)=∏iN(θi∣μi,σi2)q_\psi(\theta)=\prod_i\mathcal N(\theta_i\mid\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)qψ​(θ)=∏i​N(θi​∣μi​,σi2​), with ψ=(μ,ρ)\psi=(\mu,\rho)ψ=(μ,ρ) and σ=log⁡(1+eρ)\sigma=\log(1+e^\rho)σ=log(1+eρ) to keep σ\sigmaσ positive. The KL to a Gaussian prior is available in closed form. The expected log-likelihood is not, and we need its gradient with respect to μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ.

The reparameterization trick. Write a sample from qψq_\psiqψ​ as a deterministic function of ψ\psiψ and fixed noise:

θ=μ+σ⊙ε,ε∼N(0,I). \theta = \mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I). θ=μ+σ⊙ε,ε∼N(0,I).

Then Eqψ[g(θ)]=Eε[g(μ+σ⊙ε)]\mathbb E_{q_\psi}[g(\theta)] = \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}[g(\mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilon)]Eqψ​​[g(θ)]=Eε​[g(μ+σ⊙ε)], and since the distribution of ε\varepsilonε does not depend on ψ\psiψ, the gradient moves inside the expectation:

∇ψ Eqψ[g(θ)]=Eε[∇ψ g(μ+σ⊙ε)]≈∇ψ g(μ+σ⊙ε1), \nabla_\psi\,\mathbb E_{q_\psi}[g(\theta)] = \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_\psi\,g(\mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilon)\big]\approx\nabla_\psi\,g(\mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilon_1), ∇ψ​Eqψ​​[g(θ)]=Eε​[∇ψ​g(μ+σ⊙ε)]≈∇ψ​g(μ+σ⊙ε1​),

a single-sample, unbiased gradient that backpropagation computes like any other (Lecture 13 §1). With minibatches, scale the log-likelihood by N/BN/BN/B. This algorithm is Bayes by Backprop (Blundell et al., 2015). The reparameterization trick is also what makes VAEs trainable, next lecture.

MC dropout, revisited. Multiplying each weight row by a Bernoulli mask is itself a sample from a distribution over weights. Gal & Ghahramani (2016) showed that training with dropout and weight decay approximately maximizes an ELBO for that Bernoulli family. That is why MC dropout (§3) behaves like a crude variational posterior, and why it shares the failure modes below.

7.4 What goes wrong

  • Mode-seeking. KL(q ∥ p)\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)KL(q∥p) heavily penalizes qqq for placing mass where the posterior has little, and barely penalizes it for missing posterior mass. The optimal qqq sits inside one mode and underestimates the variance.
  • Mean-field is too restrictive. Posterior correlations between weights are strong, and a factorized Gaussian cannot represent them. For single-hidden-layer networks, mean-field Gaussian VI and MC dropout provably cannot represent increased uncertainty between separated data clusters (Foong et al., 2020) — the running example's gap.
  • Underfitting. The KL term often dominates for large networks, so practitioners down-weight it or "temper" the posterior. That improves accuracy but departs from Bayesian inference, and the resulting uncertainties are harder to interpret (Wenzel et al., 2020).
  • Cost. Twice the parameters (μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ), and noisy gradients.

In practice, mean-field VI for whole networks rarely beats a deep ensemble. Its lasting importance is the ELBO and the reparameterization trick, which become the engine of latent-variable generative models in Lecture 23.

Remember. log⁡p(D)=ELBO+KL(q ∥ p(θ∣D))\log p(\mathcal D) = \mathrm{ELBO}+\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p(\theta\mid\mathcal D))logp(D)=ELBO+KL(q∥p(θ∣D)), so maximizing the ELBO fits qqq to the posterior. The reparameterization trick θ=μ+σ⊙ε\theta=\mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilonθ=μ+σ⊙ε makes ELBO gradients ordinary backpropagation. MC dropout is VI with a Bernoulli family. Mean-field VI is mode-seeking, underestimates variance, and can miss in-between uncertainty.

8. Sampling: Langevin dynamics, SGLD and HMC (12 min)

8.1 Langevin dynamics

Instead of fitting a distribution, simulate a process whose long-run distribution is the posterior. Write π(θ)∝p(D∣θ)p(θ)\pi(\theta)\propto p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)p(\theta)π(θ)∝p(D∣θ)p(θ). The Langevin stochastic differential equation

dθt=∇θlog⁡π(θt) dt+2 dWt, d\theta_t = \nabla_\theta\log\pi(\theta_t)\,dt+\sqrt2\,dW_t, dθt​=∇θ​logπ(θt​)dt+2​dWt​,

where WtW_tWt​ is Brownian motion, has π\piπ as its stationary distribution: the gradient term pulls toward high posterior density, the noise spreads the samples, and the two balance exactly at π\piπ. Its Euler–Maruyama discretization with step ϵ\epsilonϵ is

θt+1=θt+ϵ ∇θlog⁡π(θt)+2ϵ ξt,ξt∼N(0,I). \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t+\epsilon\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi(\theta_t)+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\xi_t,\qquad\xi_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I). θt+1​=θt​+ϵ∇θ​logπ(θt​)+2ϵ​ξt​,ξt​∼N(0,I).

Without the noise, this is gradient ascent on the log posterior — MAP training. Sampling is optimization plus the right amount of noise. The same SDE, driven by a learned gradient of a log density, is how diffusion models generate samples (Lecture 24).

8.2 Stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics

Replace the full gradient by a minibatch estimate and let the step size decay (Welling & Teh, 2011):

θt+1=θt+ηt2(∇log⁡p(θt)+NB∑n∈Bt∇log⁡p(yn∣xn,θt))+N(0,ηtI). \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\frac{\eta_t}{2}\Big(\nabla\log p(\theta_t)+\frac NB\sum_{n\in\mathcal B_t}\nabla\log p(y_n\mid x_n,\theta_t)\Big)+\mathcal N(0,\eta_tI). θt+1​=θt​+2ηt​​(∇logp(θt​)+BN​n∈Bt​∑​∇logp(yn​∣xn​,θt​))+N(0,ηt​I).

This is SGD with weight decay plus Gaussian noise of a prescribed size. As ηt→0\eta_t\to0ηt​→0, the injected noise dominates the minibatch noise and the iterates sample the posterior. Compare SWAG (§5): there, the minibatch noise of constant-step SGD was used as an uncalibrated sampler; SGLD calibrates the noise so that the target is the posterior. In practice, cyclical step-size schedules help the chain move between modes (Zhang et al., 2020). Iterates are correlated, so keep every kkk-th one.

8.3 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

HMC augments θ\thetaθ with a momentum variable, simulates Hamiltonian dynamics to propose distant moves, and accepts or rejects them with a Metropolis step so that the samples are exact up to Monte Carlo error. It is the gold standard for posterior sampling, and the reference against which the other methods are judged. For neural networks it needs full-batch gradients and very many of them: Izmailov et al. (2021) used hundreds of accelerators to run it on modern image models. Their findings close the loop with §4: the HMC model average beat standard training, and deep ensembles came close to it.

Remember. Langevin dynamics dθ=∇log⁡π dt+2 dWd\theta = \nabla\log\pi\,dt+\sqrt2\,dWdθ=∇logπdt+2​dW samples π\piπ; discretized, it is gradient ascent plus noise. SGLD uses minibatch gradients and decaying steps, and calibrates the noise that SWAG uses informally. HMC is the accurate, expensive reference.

9. Evaluating uncertainty, and a map of the methods (10 min)

9.1 How to measure whether uncertainty is any good

Uncertainty estimates are predictions and must be tested like predictions (Lecture 13 §8). No single number suffices.

Criterion Measure What it catches
Overall quality Test negative log-likelihood, a proper scoring rule: minimized in expectation only by the true predictive distribution both over- and under-confidence
Calibration (regression) Coverage: fraction of test targets inside the central α\alphaα-interval, for several α\alphaα; plot observed vs nominal systematic over- or under-confidence
Calibration (classification) Reliability diagram and expected calibration error, ECE=∑bnbN∣accb−confb∣\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_b\frac{n_b}{N}\lvert\mathrm{acc}_b-\mathrm{conf}_b\rvertECE=∑b​Nnb​​∣accb​−confb​∣ over confidence bins confidence that does not match accuracy
Sharpness average predictive width, given calibration an uninformative but calibrated predictor
Epistemic signal out-of-distribution detection (AUROC using epistemic variance or mutual information as the score) uncertainty that does not grow away from the data
Usefulness downstream decisions: active-learning error vs. number of labels, BO regret, joint log-loss uncertainty that is calibrated but useless for decisions

Two warnings. Calibration alone is easy: predicting the unconditional distribution of yyy for every input is perfectly calibrated and useless. And evaluate under dataset shift, not only on held-out data from the training distribution: that is where epistemic uncertainty earns its keep and where many methods degrade (Ovadia et al., 2019). Report everything as mean ± standard deviation over seeds.

9.3 Other approaches worth knowing

  • Distance-aware single models. SNGP (Liu et al., 2020) constrains the network's Lipschitz constant with spectral normalization, so distances in feature space reflect distances in input space, and replaces the last layer by an approximate GP. This directly fixes the feature-collapse failure of §2, in one forward pass.
  • Evidential deep learning predicts the parameters of a prior distribution over the likelihood's parameters in a single pass. It is fast and popular, but its uncertainties are trained by loss minimization without a posterior to anchor them, and they have been shown not to behave like epistemic uncertainty (Bengs et al., 2022; Meinert et al., 2023). Use with caution.
  • Conformal prediction wraps any predictor and produces intervals with guaranteed coverage under exchangeability (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023). It calibrates, but it does not by itself distinguish epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty, and the guarantee is marginal over inputs. It pairs well with the methods above.

9.4 Practical guidance

  1. Start with a deep ensemble of 5, with randomized priors if out-of-distribution behavior matters.
  2. If you can only afford one training run, try a Bayesian last layer post hoc, then SWAG; use MC dropout only as a baseline.
  3. Use joint predictions — one sampled network or index across all candidate points — when the uncertainty drives sequential decisions.
  4. Evaluate with NLL, coverage, out-of-distribution tests and the downstream task, under shift, over several seeds.

P2. You fit mean-field Gaussian VI to a posterior that is a correlated 2D Gaussian with variances 1 and correlation 0.9. Will qqq overestimate or underestimate the marginal variances? Why?

Answer. Underestimate. KL(q ∥ p)\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)KL(q∥p) penalizes qqq for placing mass where ppp is small, so a factorized qqq must fit inside the narrow diagonal ridge of ppp. The optimum has variances equal to the inverse diagonal of ppp's precision matrix, 1−0.92=0.191-0.9^2=0.191−0.92=0.19, far below the true marginal variance of 1.

P3. All five members of a deep ensemble predict nearly the same value far outside the training data, and that value is wrong. Name the failure and a fix that costs nothing extra.

Answer. Ensemble collapse: members share an inductive bias that extrapolates the same way, and training gives them no reason to disagree where there is no data. Add randomized prior functions: each member gets a fixed random prior network, so away from the data the members revert to different prior functions and disagree.
