The point of this lecture. Attention looks intimidating because it usually arrives all at once, as one equation with three matrices and a softmax. It is easier to build it in steps, each of which fixes a problem from the previous one:

  1. Some tasks map a sequence to a sequence of a different length. The RNN encoder–decoder handles them, but squeezes the entire input into one vector.
  2. Fix: let the decoder look back at every input position, weighted by relevance (Bahdanau attention).
  3. "Weighted by relevance" is a soft dictionary lookup: match a query against keys, return a weighted average of values.
  4. You have seen this before. With a Gaussian weighting it is kernel regression, and with normalized inputs it becomes dot-product attention.
  5. Applied by a sequence to itself, it becomes self-attention: a layer in which every element reads from every other, with weights computed from content.
  6. Self-attention ignores order, so we add positional information, run several attentions in parallel (multi-head), and mask the future for autoregressive models.

Steps 0–4 are the core of today. Step 5 can spill into Lecture 17, where these pieces are assembled into the transformer.

0. Sequence-to-sequence: the RNN encoder–decoder and its limitations (12 min)

0.1 The task

Lecture 15's RNNs produced one output per input step (forecasting, tagging) or one output per sequence (classification). Many tasks need something else: an input sequence x1:Sx_{1:S}x1:S​ mapped to an output sequence y1:Ty_{1:T}y1:T​ of a different length, with no step-by-step correspondence. Translation is the classic case: "I like tea" has three words, "J'aime le thé" has four, and the word order need not match. Scientific versions are common:

  • Map a forcing history (a loading path, a rainfall record) to a response sampled on a different time grid.
  • Map a short window of observations to a long forecast.
  • Map a measured spectrum to the sequence of species or layers that produced it.

An RNN that emits yty_tyt​ when it reads xtx_txt​ cannot do this: it has no way to produce four outputs from three inputs, or to produce the first output before reading the last input.

0.2 The encoder–decoder

Sutskever, Vinyals and Le (2014) and Cho et al. (2014) split the job between two RNNs.

  1. Encoder. An RNN reads the whole input, hj=fenc(hj−1,xj)h_j = f_{\text{enc}}(h_{j-1},x_j)hj​=fenc​(hj−1​,xj​) for j=1,…,Sj=1,\dots,Sj=1,…,S. Its final state is the context vector m=hSm = h_Sm=hS​, a fixed-size summary of the entire input.
  2. Decoder. A second RNN, initialized with s0=ms_0=ms0​=m, generates the output autoregressively (Lecture 15 §1). At each step it takes its previous output as input,
st=fdec(st−1, yt−1),p(yt∣y<t,x1:S)=g(st), s_t = f_{\text{dec}}(s_{t-1},\,y_{t-1}),\qquad p(y_t\mid y_{<t},x_{1:S}) = g(s_t), st​=fdec​(st−1​,yt−1​),p(yt​∣y<t​,x1:S​)=g(st​),

and stops when it emits a special end-of-sequence token. The output length is decided by the model, not fixed by the input. 3. Training is maximum likelihood of the output sequence given the input, −∑tlog⁡p(yt∣y<t,x1:S)-\sum_t\log p(y_t\mid y_{<t},x_{1:S})−∑t​logp(yt​∣y<t​,x1:S​), with teacher forcing (Lecture 15 §6): the true yt−1y_{t-1}yt−1​ is fed to the decoder during training.

Picture it as x1→x2→⋯→xS⇒m⇒y1→y2→⋯→yTx_1\to x_2\to\cdots\to x_S \Rightarrow m \Rightarrow y_1\to y_2\to\cdots\to y_Tx1​→x2​→⋯→xS​⇒m⇒y1​→y2​→⋯→yT​: one pipe in, one vector in the middle, one pipe out. With deep LSTMs and large datasets this worked remarkably well for translation, and it is the template for everything in this lecture.

0.3 Three limitations

1. The bottleneck. Everything the decoder knows about the input must fit into one fixed-size vector mmm, whether the input has 5 tokens or 500. Cho et al. (2014) found that translation quality dropped sharply as sentences got longer. The scientific analogue: summarize a 10,000-step sensor history into one 256-dimensional vector, then reconstruct from it every detail the decoder needs.

2. Long paths. Information about x1x_1x1​ reaches the decoder only after passing through SSS encoder steps, and reaches yTy_TyT​ only after TTT more. By Lecture 15 §4, the gradient along that path is a product of Jacobians and tends to vanish. Gating helps but does not shorten the path. (Sutskever et al. found, tellingly, that simply reversing the input sentence improved results, because it put the first input words close to the first output words.)

3. Sequential computation. hjh_jhj​ needs hj−1h_{j-1}hj−1​, and sts_tst​ needs st−1s_{t-1}st−1​. A length-nnn sequence takes nnn steps that cannot run in parallel, on hardware built for large parallel matrix products.

Attention was invented to fix the first, and turned out to fix all three.

1. Bahdanau attention: removing the bottleneck (13 min)

1.1 The idea

Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2015) kept the RNN encoder and decoder but stopped discarding the encoder's intermediate states. The encoder produces one vector hjh_jhj​ per input position. At every output step ttt, the decoder builds its own context vector from all of them, weighted by how relevant each is right now:

etj=score⁡(st−1, hj),αtj=exp⁡(etj)∑j′=1Sexp⁡(etj′),ct=∑j=1Sαtj hj. e_{tj} = \operatorname{score}(s_{t-1},\,h_j), \qquad \alpha_{tj} = \frac{\exp(e_{tj})}{\sum_{j'=1}^{S}\exp(e_{tj'})}, \qquad c_t = \sum_{j=1}^{S}\alpha_{tj}\,h_j . etj​=score(st−1​,hj​),αtj​=∑j′=1S​exp(etj′​)exp(etj​)​,ct​=j=1∑S​αtj​hj​.

The decoder then updates its state using ctc_tct​ as an additional input, st=f(st−1, yt−1, ct)s_t = f(s_{t-1},\,y_{t-1},\,c_t)st​=f(st−1​,yt−1​,ct​), and predicts yty_tyt​ from sts_tst​ and ctc_tct​.

In the original paper the score is a small MLP, score⁡(s,h)=v⊤tanh⁡(Ws+Uh)\operatorname{score}(s,h) = v^\top\tanh(W s + U h)score(s,h)=v⊤tanh(Ws+Uh) (additive attention). Luong et al. (2015) showed a simpler bilinear score, s⊤Whs^\top W hs⊤Wh (multiplicative attention), works as well and is cheaper. Everything is differentiable and trained end to end by ordinary maximum likelihood.

1.2 Reading the weights

For each output step, (αt1,…,αtS)(\alpha_{t1},\dots,\alpha_{tS})(αt1​,…,αtS​) is a probability distribution over input positions: where the decoder is "looking." Plot α\alphaα as a matrix — outputs on one axis, inputs on the other — and for translation you see a learned alignment. It is mostly diagonal, since word order is mostly preserved, with off-diagonal blocks where it is not. In English–French translation, for instance, adjective–noun order flips ("European Economic Area" becomes "zone économique européenne"), and the weights show the decoder looking at "Area" first. Nobody provided alignment labels; they emerge because they reduce the loss.

1.3 What it fixed

Limitation (§0) With attention
Bottleneck Gone: the context ctc_tct​ is recomputed at every step from all SSS encoder states
Long paths Every input position is one weighted sum away from every output step
Sequential computation Still there: the encoder and decoder are still RNNs

The third row is the opening for Vaswani et al. (2017): if attention already provides every connection that matters, do we need the recurrence at all? Before answering, we strip attention down to what it actually computes.

Remember. Bahdanau attention: at each output step, score every encoder state against the current decoder state, softmax the scores into weights, and take the weighted average as context. The weights are a learned soft alignment.

2. Attention as a soft dictionary lookup (12 min)

2.1 Three roles

Strip away the RNNs, and §1.1 does the following. Something asks a question (the decoder state). A collection of items each advertise what they contain (the encoder states, used in the score). Each item also carries content to hand back (the encoder states again, averaged into ctc_tct​). Give the three roles names:

  • Query qqq: what I am looking for.
  • Keys k1,…,kmk_1,\dots,k_mk1​,…,km​: what each item advertises, used for matching.
  • Values v1,…,vmv_1,\dots,v_mv1​,…,vm​: what each item returns if selected.

Attention is

Attn⁡(q; K,V)=∑j=1mαj vj,αj=softmax⁡j(score⁡(q,kj)). \operatorname{Attn}(q;\,K,V) = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_j\,v_j, \qquad \alpha_j = \operatorname{softmax}_j\big(\operatorname{score}(q,k_j)\big). Attn(q;K,V)=j=1∑m​αj​vj​,αj​=softmaxj​(score(q,kj​)).

In Bahdanau's model, the keys and the values are the same vectors hjh_jhj​. Separating them is useful because what you match on need not be what you retrieve. A library catalog is searched by title and subject (keys), but what you take home is the book (values). A network of sensors is searched by location and type (keys), but what you want back is the reading (values).

2.2 Hard versus soft lookup

A Python dictionary does a hard lookup: find the one key that matches the query exactly and return its value. That is not differentiable — a small change in the query either changes nothing or jumps to a different key — so it cannot be learned by gradient descent.

Attention does a soft lookup. Every key matches to some degree, the softmax turns degrees of match into weights that sum to one, and the result is a blend of all the values. As the scores sharpen, the softmax approaches a one-hot vector and the soft lookup approaches the hard one. Because every step is differentiable, the network can learn what to ask for (queries), how to advertise (keys) and what to return (values).

Remember. Attention = soft dictionary lookup. Compare the query with every key, softmax the scores into weights, return the weighted average of the values. Keys are for matching; values are for returning.

3. You have seen this before: kernel regression (8 min)

3.1 Nadaraya–Watson

The example in §2.3 is a classical estimator. Given data (xj,yj)(x_j,y_j)(xj​,yj​), the Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression estimate (Nadaraya, 1964; Watson, 1964) at a point x⋆x^\starx⋆ is

f^(x⋆)=∑j=1mκ(x⋆,xj)∑j′κ(x⋆,xj′) yj. \hat f(x^\star) = \sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\kappa(x^\star,x_j)}{\sum_{j'}\kappa(x^\star,x_{j'})}\,y_j . f^​(x⋆)=j=1∑m​∑j′​κ(x⋆,xj′​)κ(x⋆,xj​)​yj​.

With a Gaussian kernel κ(x,x′)=exp⁡(−∥x−x′∥2/2ℓ2)\kappa(x,x') = \exp\big(-\lVert x-x'\rVert^2/2\ell^2\big)κ(x,x′)=exp(−∥x−x′∥2/2ℓ2), the normalized weights are exactly a softmax:

κ(x⋆,xj)∑j′κ(x⋆,xj′)=softmax⁡j(−∥x⋆−xj∥22ℓ2). \frac{\kappa(x^\star,x_j)}{\sum_{j'}\kappa(x^\star,x_{j'})} = \operatorname{softmax}_j\Big(-\frac{\lVert x^\star-x_j\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}\Big). ∑j′​κ(x⋆,xj′​)κ(x⋆,xj​)​=softmaxj​(−2ℓ2∥x⋆−xj​∥2​).

Nadaraya–Watson regression is attention with query x⋆x^\starx⋆, keys xjx_jxj​, values yjy_jyj​, and a Gaussian score.

Compare the GP predictive mean of Lecture 9, μ(x⋆)=k(x⋆,X) (K+σ2I)−1y\mu(x^\star) = k(x^\star,X)\,(K+\sigma^2I)^{-1}yμ(x⋆)=k(x⋆,X)(K+σ2I)−1y. It is also a weighted sum of training targets, but its weights account for correlations between training points through the inverse, and they need not be positive or sum to one. Nadaraya–Watson skips the inverse and simply normalizes.

What attention adds is learning: the kernel is not fixed in advance but parameterized, and queries and keys are produced by learned maps.

3.2 From the Gaussian kernel to the dot product

Expand the Gaussian score:

−∥q−kj∥22ℓ2=−∥q∥22ℓ2+q⊤kjℓ2−∥kj∥22ℓ2. -\frac{\lVert q-k_j\rVert^2}{2\ell^2} = -\frac{\lVert q\rVert^2}{2\ell^2} + \frac{q^\top k_j}{\ell^2} - \frac{\lVert k_j\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}. −2ℓ2∥q−kj​∥2​=−2ℓ2∥q∥2​+ℓ2q⊤kj​​−2ℓ2∥kj​∥2​.
  1. The first term is the same for every jjj. A softmax is unchanged by adding a constant to all its inputs, so the term drops out.
  2. If all keys have the same norm, the third term is also constant across jjj and drops out.
  3. What remains is
αj=softmax⁡j(q⊤kjℓ2). \alpha_j = \operatorname{softmax}_j\Big(\frac{q^\top k_j}{\ell^2}\Big). αj​=softmaxj​(ℓ2q⊤kj​​).

Dot-product attention is Gaussian kernel smoothing on normalized keys, with ℓ2\ell^2ℓ2 playing the role of a temperature. The dot product is a similarity: large when qqq and kjk_jkj​ point the same way. It is also cheap: all query–key pairs together are a single matrix product.

Remember. Attention generalizes kernel regression: a softmax over similarities, applied to values. With a Gaussian kernel it is Nadaraya–Watson; with normalized keys the Gaussian score becomes a dot product.

4. Scaled dot-product attention (10 min)

4.1 Matrix form

Stack nnn queries as rows of Q∈Rn×dkQ\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}Q∈Rn×dk​, and mmm keys and values as rows of K∈Rm×dkK\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_k}K∈Rm×dk​ and V∈Rm×dvV\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_v}V∈Rm×dv​. Row iii of the output is query iii's soft lookup. This is the equation of Vaswani et al. (2017). Every part of it is now familiar except the dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​.

4.2 Why divide by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​

Suppose the entries of qqq and kkk are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, as they roughly are at a good initialization (Lecture 13). Then

Var⁡[q⊤k]=∑i=1dkVar⁡[qiki]=dk. \operatorname{Var}\big[q^\top k\big] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k}\operatorname{Var}[q_ik_i] = d_k . Var[q⊤k]=i=1∑dk​​Var[qi​ki​]=dk​.

With dk=64d_k=64dk​=64, raw scores have standard deviation 8, so typical gaps between scores are of order 10 or more. The softmax of such scores is nearly one-hot, and a saturated softmax has nearly zero gradient — the saturation problem of Lecture 13 in a new place. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ restores unit variance, so the softmax starts in its responsive regime whatever the width. In the language of §3, it fixes the temperature.

5. Self-attention (15 min)

5.1 A sequence attending to itself

In Bahdanau attention the queries come from one sequence (the decoder) and the keys and values from another (the encoder): cross-attention. In self-attention, all three come from the same sequence, through three learned projections:

Q=XWQ,K=XWK,V=XWV,Y=softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk)V. Q = XW_Q,\qquad K = XW_K,\qquad V = XW_V,\qquad Y = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)V . Q=XWQ​,K=XWK​,V=XWV​,Y=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​)V.

Every token issues a query, every token offers a key and a value, and every token's output yiy_iyi​ is a weighted average of all tokens' values. The output has one row per input token — a new representation of each token, informed by every other.

The three projections let a token ask for something different from what it advertises. In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired," a useful representation of "it" should borrow from "animal." Its query can look for "a noun this pronoun could refer to," while "animal" advertises, through its key, "an animate noun." Trained models do learn heads with this behavior.

5.3 How self-attention differs from convolution and recurrence

Convolution ∑jwj−i xj\sum_j w_{j-i}\,x_j∑j​wj−i​xj​ fixed, shared, depend only on the offset, local
Recurrence hi=f(hi−1,xi)h_i = f(h_{i-1},x_i)hi​=f(hi−1​,xi​) implicit, passed step by step through a state
Self-attention ∑jαij(X) vj\sum_j\alpha_{ij}(X)\,v_j∑j​αij​(X)vj​ computed from the content, global

A convolution applies the same stencil everywhere, whatever the data. Self-attention recomputes its "stencil" for every input: which positions matter, and how much, depends on what is in them. It is global — position 1 can read position 1000 in one step — and since all rows are computed by matrix products, it is fully parallel over the sequence. Of the three limitations in §0, the bottleneck and the long paths were fixed by attention; sequential computation is fixed by dropping recurrence altogether.

Scientific examples.

  • Interacting particles. Treat each atom or particle as a token. Self-attention computes a learned, state-dependent interaction weight between every pair — a learned analogue of a pairwise force law, with no cutoff radius fixed in advance.
  • Sparse sensors to a field. Use cross-attention with queries at the locations where you want the field and keys and values from the sensors (positions and readings). This is the Nadaraya–Watson interpolation of §3 with a learned kernel, and several neural operators decode fields in exactly this way.
  • Global coupling on a grid. A CNN needs many layers to connect distant points of a field (Lecture 14 §4). One self-attention layer connects them directly, which matters for problems with long-range coupling such as pressure in incompressible flow.

5.4 Self-attention ignores order

Permute the tokens: replace XXX by PXPXPX for a permutation matrix PPP. Then QQQ, KKK, VVV become PQPQPQ, PKPKPK, PVPVPV, and

softmax⁡ ⁣(PQ(PK)⊤dk)PV=Psoftmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk)P⊤PV=P Y, \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{PQ(PK)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)PV = P\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)P^\top PV = P\,Y, softmax(dk​​PQ(PK)⊤​)PV=Psoftmax(dk​​QK⊤​)P⊤PV=PY,

using P⊤P=IP^\top P = IP⊤P=I and the fact that permuting the rows and columns of a matrix permutes the rows of its row-wise softmax in the same way. Self-attention is permutation equivariant: shuffle the inputs and the outputs shuffle identically, with no other change. It treats its input as a set.

That is exactly right for a set of particles, and exactly wrong for a sentence or a time series: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the same tokens. Compare Lecture 14, where convolution was equivariant to translations — a smaller symmetry group, which preserves relative position. Self-attention is equivariant to all permutations, which preserves nothing about position. When order matters, it must be supplied (§6).

Remember. Self-attention: QQQ, KKK, VVV are three projections of the same sequence; every output is a content-weighted average over all positions. Unlike convolution, its weights depend on the data and are global; unlike recurrence, it is parallel. It is permutation equivariant, so it knows nothing about order by itself.

6. Positional information (8 min)

Since self-attention sees a set, we add position to each token's content before the first layer: xi←xi+pix_i \leftarrow x_i + p_ixi​←xi​+pi​, where pi∈Rdp_i\in\mathbb{R}^dpi​∈Rd encodes position iii.

Learned positional embeddings are one trainable vector per position. Simple, but they say nothing about positions never seen in training.

Sinusoidal encodings (Vaswani et al., 2017) use sines and cosines at geometrically spaced frequencies:

pi,2r=sin⁡(i ωr),pi,2r+1=cos⁡(i ωr),ωr=10000−2r/d. p_{i,2r} = \sin\big(i\,\omega_r\big),\qquad p_{i,2r+1}=\cos\big(i\,\omega_r\big),\qquad \omega_r = 10000^{-2r/d}. pi,2r​=sin(iωr​),pi,2r+1​=cos(iωr​),ωr​=10000−2r/d.

Low-frequency components distinguish distant positions; high-frequency components distinguish neighbors. For each frequency, the pair (sin⁡iω,cos⁡iω)(\sin i\omega,\cos i\omega)(siniω,cosiω) at position i+Δi+\Deltai+Δ is a fixed rotation of the pair at position iii, so relative offsets are easy for linear maps to read off.

These are the Fourier features that cure spectral bias in coordinate networks (Lecture 14 §0), and the parallel is direct: in scientific problems, the "position" of a token is often a physical coordinate (x,y,z,t)(x,y,z,t)(x,y,z,t), and a Fourier-feature embedding of that coordinate is the natural positional encoding. Many current models instead inject relative position directly into the scores (for example, rotary embeddings, which rotate queries and keys by position-dependent angles), so that attention depends on i−ji-ji−j rather than on iii and jjj separately — translation equivariance recovered.

Remember. Self-attention is order-blind, so position is added to the input. Sinusoidal encodings are Fourier features of the position; for physical data, Fourier features of the coordinates play the same role.

7. Multi-head attention and causal masking (10 min)

7.1 Several lookups in parallel

One attention layer gives each token a single weighted average. But a token may need several kinds of information at once: a word needs its subject and its neighbors; a particle feels short-range repulsion and long-range attraction. Multi-head attention runs hhh attentions in parallel, each with its own projections and a reduced width dk=d/hd_k = d/hdk​=d/h, concatenates the results, and mixes them with one more matrix:

headr=Attention⁡(XWQ(r), XWK(r), XWV(r)),MHA⁡(X)=[head1,…,headh] WO. \text{head}_r = \operatorname{Attention}\big(XW_Q^{(r)},\,XW_K^{(r)},\,XW_V^{(r)}\big),\qquad \operatorname{MHA}(X) = \big[\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h\big]\,W_O . headr​=Attention(XWQ(r)​,XWK(r)​,XWV(r)​),MHA(X)=[head1​,…,headh​]WO​.

Because each head has width d/hd/hd/h, the total cost is about that of one full-width head. Different heads learn different patterns: in language, some track adjacent words, some syntax, some coreference.

7.2 Causal masking

For autoregressive generation (Lecture 15 §1), position iii must not see positions j>ij>ij>i: the model of p(yi∣y<i)p(y_i\mid y_{<i})p(yi​∣y<i​) cannot use yiy_iyi​ or anything after it. Enforce this by setting those scores to −∞-\infty−∞ before the softmax:

softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk+M)V,Mij={0j≤i−∞j>i. \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\Big)V,\qquad M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0 & j\le i\\ -\infty & j>i\end{cases}. softmax(dk​​QK⊤​+M)V,Mij​={0−∞​j≤ij>i​.

e−∞=0e^{-\infty}=0e−∞=0, so future positions get zero weight, and the weight matrix is lower triangular.

This one line resolves the tension of Lecture 15 §6. With teacher forcing, an RNN still had to step through the sequence one position at a time. A causally masked self-attention layer computes all nnn next-token predictions in a single parallel pass, each one seeing exactly its own past — teacher-forced maximum likelihood for the whole sequence at once. This is why transformers train so much faster than RNNs on long sequences.

7.3 The price

Every query is compared with every key, so the cost per layer is O(n2d)O(n^2d)O(n2d) time and O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) memory for the weights. For a sentence of a few hundred tokens this is negligible. For a 256×256256\times256256×256 field treated as one token per grid point, n=65,536n = 65{,}536n=65,536 and each head of each layer computes n2≈4.3×109n^2\approx4.3\times10^9n2≈4.3×109 scores. Grouping grid points into patches, restricting attention to local windows, and approximating the softmax are the main remedies, and they come up again when we reach transformers for images and neural operators.

Remember. Multi-head: several smaller attentions in parallel, concatenated and mixed. Causal mask: −∞-\infty−∞ on future scores, which lets an autoregressive model train on all positions in parallel. Cost grows as n2n^2n2.

8. Summary (2 min)

Attention began as a fix for the RNN encoder–decoder's bottleneck: score every encoder state against the decoder's current state, softmax, and average. Stripped down, it is a soft dictionary lookup — queries, keys, values — and with a Gaussian score it is the Nadaraya–Watson kernel smoother, which becomes dot-product attention on normalized keys. Scaled by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ and written in matrix form, it is one line. Applied by a sequence to itself, it gives a layer whose mixing weights are computed from the data, connect all positions directly, and can be computed in parallel. It is blind to order, so position is added; it is run in several heads at once; and with a causal mask it trains an autoregressive model on all positions at once.

Next lecture assembles these pieces — attention, the MLPs of Lecture 12, and the residual connections and normalization of Lecture 13 — into the transformer block.

Quiz-eligible facts

  1. An RNN encoder–decoder maps an input sequence to an output sequence of different length: the encoder compresses the input into a context vector m=hSm=h_Sm=hS​, and the decoder generates the output autoregressively from it. It has three limitations: a fixed-size context bottleneck, long gradient paths between distant positions, and sequential computation.
  2. Bahdanau attention computes, at each output step, αtj=softmax⁡j(score⁡(st−1,hj))\alpha_{tj}=\operatorname{softmax}_j(\operatorname{score}(s_{t-1},h_j))αtj​=softmaxj​(score(st−1​,hj​)) and ct=∑jαtjhjc_t=\sum_j\alpha_{tj}h_jct​=∑j​αtj​hj​; the weights form a learned soft alignment and sum to one over input positions.
  3. Attention is a soft dictionary lookup: queries are matched against keys, and the output is a softmax-weighted average of values.
  4. Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression with a Gaussian kernel is attention with query x⋆x^\starx⋆, keys xjx_jxj​ and values yjy_jyj​; with keys of equal norm, the Gaussian score reduces to a scaled dot product.
  5. Scaled dot-product attention: softmax⁡(QK⊤/dk)V\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})Vsoftmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V; the scaling keeps score variance at 1, because Var⁡[q⊤k]=dk\operatorname{Var}[q^\top k]=d_kVar[q⊤k]=dk​ for unit-variance entries, and prevents softmax saturation.
  6. Self-attention computes QQQ, KKK, VVV from the same sequence; cross-attention takes queries from one sequence and keys and values from another.
  7. Unlike convolution, self-attention's mixing weights depend on content and connect all positions; unlike recurrence, it is computed in parallel.
  8. Self-attention is permutation equivariant, f(PX)=Pf(X)f(PX)=Pf(X)f(PX)=Pf(X), so positional information must be added; sinusoidal encodings are Fourier features of position.
  9. Multi-head attention runs hhh attentions of width d/hd/hd/h in parallel and mixes their concatenation with WOW_OWO​.
  10. A causal mask sets future scores to −∞-\infty−∞, giving a lower-triangular weight matrix and allowing all next-token predictions to be trained in one parallel pass.
  11. Self-attention costs O(n2d)O(n^2d)O(n2d) time and O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) memory in the sequence length nnn.

P3. With dk=256d_k=256dk​=256 and unit-variance query and key entries, what is the standard deviation of an unscaled score q⊤kq^\top kq⊤k? Why does this hurt training?

Answer. 256=16\sqrt{256}=16256​=16. Score gaps of this size make the softmax nearly one-hot, where its gradient is nearly zero, so the projections that produce queries and keys barely learn. Dividing by dk=16\sqrt{d_k}=16dk​​=16 restores unit variance.

P4. You feed the token sequences "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" to a single self-attention layer with no positional encodings. How are the two outputs related? What does this imply?

Answer. The second input is a permutation of the first, so its outputs are the same vectors in the permuted order: "dog" gets the same representation in both sentences. The layer cannot distinguish the meanings, which is why positional information must be added.
