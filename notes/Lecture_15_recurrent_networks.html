The point of this lecture. CNNs share weights across space because the same local pattern means the same thing everywhere. Recurrent networks share weights across time because the same dynamics act at every step. An RNN is a learned discrete-time dynamical system, and nearly everything that makes RNNs useful — and hard to train — follows from that view. We end by locating the RNN's two structural limitations, an information bottleneck and strictly sequential computation, which are exactly what attention and the transformer remove.

0. Why sequences, and why science cares (8 min)

Every model so far has mapped a fixed-size input to a fixed-size output. Many problems do not fit that mold: the input, the output, or both are sequences of variable length, and the order matters.

In machine learning the classic examples are language modeling (the distribution over text), speech recognition, machine translation, and captioning. Science and engineering supply at least as many:

  • Forecasting dynamical systems. Predict the next state of a turbulent flow, a power grid, or a chaotic oscillator from its recent history. Echo state networks, a simple RNN variant (§5.4), forecast the chaotic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation several Lyapunov times ahead from data alone (Pathak et al., 2018).
  • Hydrology. LSTMs trained on rainfall and meteorological series from 531 US catchments predicted river discharge better than calibrated conceptual hydrological models (Kratzert et al., 2019).
  • Path-dependent materials. Stress in a plastic material depends on the entire loading history, not on the current strain. RNNs trained on simulated loading paths learn this history dependence and serve as surrogate constitutive laws (Mozaffar et al., 2019).
  • Monitoring and diagnosis. Vibration signals for structural health monitoring, ECG and EEG traces, battery voltage curves for predicting remaining life.
  • Spatiotemporal fields. Replacing the matrix products in an LSTM with convolutions gives the ConvLSTM, introduced for precipitation nowcasting from radar sequences (Shi et al., 2015).

What these share is that the past matters, and that how much of the past matters is not known in advance.

1. The probabilistic formulation: autoregressive models and state (10 min)

1.1 The autoregressive factorization

By the chain rule of probability (Lecture 3), any distribution over a sequence factorizes exactly as

p(y1:T)=∏t=1Tp(yt∣y1:t−1). p(y_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(y_t\mid y_{1:t-1}). p(y1:T​)=t=1∏T​p(yt​∣y1:t−1​).

Modeling a sequence reduces to modeling the next-step conditional. If inputs x1:Tx_{1:T}x1:T​ are also present (forcing, controls, other sensors), condition on them as well: p(yt∣y1:t−1,x1:t)p(y_t\mid y_{1:t-1},x_{1:t})p(yt​∣y1:t−1​,x1:t​). The likelihood choice is the same as before: Gaussian for continuous signals (squared-error loss), categorical for tokens (cross-entropy), and maximum likelihood minimizes −∑tlog⁡p(yt∣y<t)-\sum_t\log p(y_t\mid y_{<t})−∑t​logp(yt​∣y<t​).

The difficulty is that the conditioning set grows with ttt. There are two ways to handle it.

1.2 Option 1: a fixed window

Assume the next step depends only on the last kkk steps: p(yt∣yt−k:t−1)p(y_t\mid y_{t-k:t-1})p(yt​∣yt−k:t−1​). This is a kkk-th order Markov model (the MCMC lectures used k=1k=1k=1). Any fixed-input model can implement it — an MLP on the concatenated window, or a 1D convolution over time (Lecture 14), which shares weights across time positions. Linear versions are the AR(kkk) models of classical time-series analysis.

The limitation is that kkk must be chosen in advance, cost grows with kkk, and nothing before the window can matter. A material that yielded 500 steps ago is invisible to a 100-step window.

1.3 Option 2: a state

Classical physics and control have long used a different idea: summarize the entire past in a state that is updated at each step,

ht=f(ht−1, xt),y^t=g(ht). h_t = f(h_{t-1},\,x_t), \qquad \hat y_t = g(h_t). ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​),y^​t​=g(ht​).

Given hth_tht​, the past adds nothing — the state is a sufficient statistic for prediction. This is a discrete-time state-space model: a numerical integrator for an ODE, a Kalman filter, an internal-variable model of plasticity (where hth_tht​ holds the plastic strain and hardening variables). A recurrent neural network learns fff and ggg from data. Its memory is unbounded in principle, its cost per step is constant, and the same parameters handle sequences of any length.

2. The vanilla RNN (14 min)

2.1 The model

The simplest choice, due to Elman (1990), is an MLP layer that takes the previous state as an additional input:

ht=tanh⁡(Wht−1+Uxt+b),y^t=Vht+c,h0=0. h_t = \tanh\big(Wh_{t-1}+Ux_t+b\big), \qquad \hat y_t = Vh_t + c, \qquad h_0 = 0. ht​=tanh(Wht−1​+Uxt​+b),y^​t​=Vht​+c,h0​=0.

The parameters θ={W,U,b,V,c}\theta=\{W,U,b,V,c\}θ={W,U,b,V,c} are shared across all time steps: H2+HD+HH^2+HD+HH2+HD+H for the recurrence plus the readout. This is the temporal analogue of Lecture 14's weight sharing, with the same justification: the dynamics are the same at every step (time-translation invariance, or autonomy, in the language of dynamical systems).

2.2 Unrolling

For a sequence of length TTT, the recurrence unrolls into a feedforward network of depth TTT in which every layer has the same weights:

h0→  W,U  h1→  W,U  h2→  W,U  ⋯→  W,U  hT,xt entering at every step,y^t leaving at every step. h_0 \xrightarrow{\;W,U\;} h_1 \xrightarrow{\;W,U\;} h_2 \xrightarrow{\;W,U\;} \cdots \xrightarrow{\;W,U\;} h_T, \qquad x_t \text{ entering at every step}, \quad \hat y_t \text{ leaving at every step}. h0​W,U​h1​W,U​h2​W,U​⋯W,U​hT​,xt​ entering at every step,y^​t​ leaving at every step.

Two consequences carry through the rest of the lecture. Everything from Lecture 13 applies to the unrolled network, so training is reverse-mode AD on it (§3). And that network is deep — as deep as the sequence is long — which is where the difficulties come from (§4).

Two common extensions: stacked RNNs, where the state sequence of one layer is the input sequence of the next, and bidirectional RNNs, which run a second RNN backward in time and concatenate the states. Bidirectional models see the future, so they suit offline tasks such as labeling a recorded signal, not forecasting.

2.4 Worked example: learning a sine wave

Take y(t)=sin⁡(πt)y(t) = \sin(\pi t)y(t)=sin(πt) sampled at spacing Δt\Delta tΔt, and train an RNN to predict yty_tyt​ from yt−1y_{t-1}yt−1​ (so xt=yt−1x_t = y_{t-1}xt​=yt−1​), with a Gaussian likelihood:

L(θ)=1T∑t=1T(yt−y^t)2. \mathcal L(\theta) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(y_t-\hat y_t\big)^2 . L(θ)=T1​t=1∑T​(yt​−y^​t​)2.

A first-order Markov model cannot solve this, because yt−1y_{t-1}yt−1​ alone does not determine whether the wave is rising or falling. Two lags suffice: the linear recurrence yt=2cos⁡(πΔt) yt−1−yt−2y_t = 2\cos(\pi\Delta t)\,y_{t-1} - y_{t-2}yt​=2cos(πΔt)yt−1​−yt−2​ holds exactly for a sinusoid. An RNN with a 2D state can learn the equivalent rotation in its hidden space — the state encodes phase, which the input alone does not reveal.

If, instead of running the RNN over the whole sequence, you reset h=0h=0h=0 every two steps, you get a model that sees only two lags: a windowed model (§1.2) written in recurrent form. The power of the RNN comes from carrying the state forward across the entire sequence.

Remember. An RNN is ht=tanh⁡(Wht−1+Uxt+b)h_t = \tanh(Wh_{t-1}+Ux_t+b)ht​=tanh(Wht−1​+Uxt​+b) with the same weights at every step: a learned discrete dynamical system. Unrolled, it is a deep feedforward network with shared weights, and the likelihood determines the loss exactly as before.

This is the same accumulation as the convolution kernel gradient summing over positions (Lecture 14 §5), now over time steps.

Cost and memory. One forward and one backward sweep, each O(T)O(T)O(T) steps, and every hth_tht​ must be stored — memory grows linearly with sequence length. For long sequences, truncated BPTT splits the sequence into chunks of length τ\tauτ: the state is carried forward across chunk boundaries, but gradients are not propagated back past them. Memory becomes O(τ)O(\tau)O(τ), at the price of never learning dependencies longer than τ\tauτ steps from the gradient.

The steps cannot be parallelized. hth_tht​ needs ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​, which needs ht−2h_{t-2}ht−2​, and so on. A CNN processes all positions of an image at once; an RNN must take TTT sequential steps, forward and backward. On GPUs built for large parallel matrix products, this is the RNN's most expensive property. It is the first of the two reasons we will eventually replace recurrence with attention.

4. Vanishing and exploding gradients: the dynamical-systems view (12 min)

4.1 The product of Jacobians

How much does the loss at step TTT depend on the state at an earlier step kkk? By the chain rule,

∂hT∂hk=∏t=k+1TJt,Jt=∂ht∂ht−1=diag⁡(1−ht2) W. \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{t=k+1}^{T}J_t, \qquad J_t = \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} = \operatorname{diag}\big(1-h_t^2\big)\,W . ∂hk​∂hT​​=t=k+1∏T​Jt​,Jt​=∂ht−1​∂ht​​=diag(1−ht2​)W.

This is Lecture 13 §2's product of layer Jacobians, with one crucial difference: the same matrix WWW appears in every factor. In a feedforward network each layer has its own weights, and their effects can partly cancel. In an RNN, whatever WWW does, it does T−kT-kT−k times.

4.2 The bound

Let γ=sup⁡∣tanh⁡′∣=1\gamma = \sup|\tanh'| = 1γ=sup∣tanh′∣=1 and let smax⁡s_{\max}smax​ be the largest singular value of WWW. Then

∥∂hT∂hk∥≤∏t=k+1T∥diag⁡(1−ht2)∥ ∥W∥≤(γ smax⁡)T−k. \Big\lVert\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k}\Big\rVert \le \prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\lVert\operatorname{diag}(1-h_t^2)\rVert\,\lVert W\rVert \le \big(\gamma\,s_{\max}\big)^{T-k}. ∥∥​∂hk​∂hT​​∥∥​≤t=k+1∏T​∥diag(1−ht2​)∥∥W∥≤(γsmax​)T−k.

If smax⁡<1/γs_{\max}<1/\gammasmax​<1/γ, the gradient vanishes exponentially in the time lag. Conversely, smax⁡>1/γs_{\max}>1/\gammasmax​>1/γ is necessary (though not sufficient, since saturation shrinks the tanh⁡′\tanh'tanh′ factors) for it to explode (Pascanu et al., 2013).

The linear case makes this concrete. With ht=Wht−1h_t = Wh_{t-1}ht​=Wht−1​ and W=QΛQ−1W = Q\Lambda Q^{-1}W=QΛQ−1,

∂hT∂hk=WT−k=Q ΛT−kQ−1: \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k} = W^{T-k} = Q\,\Lambda^{T-k}Q^{-1}: ∂hk​∂hT​​=WT−k=QΛT−kQ−1:

every eigendirection is multiplied by λiT−k\lambda_i^{T-k}λiT−k​. Components with ∣λi∣<1|\lambda_i|<1∣λi​∣<1 are forgotten exponentially fast; components with ∣λi∣>1|\lambda_i|>1∣λi​∣>1 blow up. The window in which information survives without blowing up is ∣λi∣≈1|\lambda_i|\approx1∣λi​∣≈1.

4.3 What it means

Vanishing gradients mean the network cannot learn long-range dependencies. The gradient contribution from an event T−kT-kT−k steps back decays like (γsmax⁡)T−k(\gamma s_{\max})^{T-k}(γsmax​)T−k. Short-range terms dominate the sum in §3, step 4, so the network learns short-term structure and fails to learn that a load cycle 200 steps ago determines today's stress. The information may be present in hth_tht​; the gradient just never tells the network to keep it.

Exploding gradients mean training is unstable. A single long sequence can produce a gradient orders of magnitude larger than usual and destroy many steps of progress.

This is a statement about stability. View ht=f(ht−1,xt)h_t = f(h_{t-1},x_t)ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​) as a dynamical system. If it is contractive — nearby states converge — the system forgets its initial conditions, and the gradient with respect to them vanishes. If it is expansive, nearby states diverge and the gradient explodes. A trainable RNN must sit near the boundary, retaining information without amplifying it.

For chaotic systems, exploding gradients are the truth, not a pathology. If the RNN correctly reproduces chaotic dynamics with largest Lyapunov exponent λ>0\lambda>0λ>0, then perturbations really do grow like eλte^{\lambda t}eλt, and so does ∂hT/∂hk\partial h_T/\partial h_k∂hT​/∂hk​. No architecture removes this; the true sensitivity of a long trajectory to its initial state is exponential (Mikhaeil et al., 2022). The practical consequence: train chaotic forecasters on horizons of a few Lyapunov times, not on long rollouts, and expect predictability to be bounded by the physics, not by the model.

5. Gated RNNs: LSTM and GRU (14 min)

5.1 The idea

The vanilla RNN overwrites its whole state at every step: hth_tht​ is a fresh nonlinear function of ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​. Lecture 13 showed how to keep gradients alive through depth: make each layer compute a correction, h+F(h)h+F(h)h+F(h), so the backward product contains an identity path. Gated RNNs do the same through time, and they let the network decide, input by input, how much to keep.

5.4 A gradient-free alternative: echo state networks

Vanishing and exploding gradients come from training WWW through BPTT. Reservoir computing sidesteps both: draw WWW and UUU at random, scale WWW to a spectral radius slightly below 1 so that the dynamics are stable but slowly forgetting (the "echo state property"), and train only the linear readout VVV by ridge regression — the closed form of Lecture 5. It is Lecture 12's random-features model, in time. Echo state networks are surprisingly strong at forecasting chaotic systems (Pathak et al., 2018), and they make an excellent baseline (Code Checkpoint 15C).

Remember. GRU: a leaky integrator with a learned, input-dependent time constant. Initialize the forget bias near 1.

6. Training for rollout: teacher forcing and its failure mode (8 min)

For forecasting, the model is trained to predict yty_tyt​ from the true past, but used to predict from its own past.

Teacher forcing. During training, feed the ground-truth yt−1y_{t-1}yt−1​ as the input at step ttt. This is exactly maximum likelihood under the autoregressive factorization of §1.1, every step's loss can be computed in parallel given the true inputs, and training is stable.

Free-running rollout. At test time the true yt−1y_{t-1}yt−1​ is not available, so the model feeds back its own prediction y^t−1\hat y_{t-1}y^​t−1​. Now the inputs come from a distribution the model never saw in training — its own slightly wrong outputs. This mismatch is called exposure bias. Errors compound: a model with 1% one-step error can be useless after 100 steps, and for chaotic dynamics the error grows like eλte^{\lambda t}eλt however accurate the one-step model is.

Remedies, all used in learned simulators:

  • Multi-step (rollout) loss. Train on kkk-step free-running rollouts, backpropagating through the model's own predictions. This optimizes what you actually use, at the cost of longer, more sequential computation and the gradient issues of §4.
  • Scheduled sampling (Bengio et al., 2015). Gradually replace true inputs with model predictions during training.
  • Noise injection. Corrupt training inputs with noise comparable to the model's own errors, so that it learns to correct drift rather than amplify it (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020).

Evaluate what you deploy. Report rollout error against horizon, not just one-step error. For chaotic systems, report the valid prediction time — how long the rollout stays within a tolerance of the truth — in units of the Lyapunov time 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ. A one-step metric alone hides the failure that matters.

7. Encoder–decoder models and the road to attention (12 min)

7.1 Sequence-to-sequence

When input and output sequences have different lengths and no alignment — translating a sentence, or mapping a forcing history to a response sampled differently — use two RNNs (Sutskever et al., 2014; Cho et al., 2014). An encoder reads x1:Sx_{1:S}x1:S​ and its final state becomes a context vector m=hSencm = h^{\text{enc}}_Sm=hSenc​. A decoder, initialized from mmm, generates y1,y2,…y_1,y_2,\dotsy1​,y2​,… autoregressively, one step at a time.

7.2 The bottleneck

Everything the decoder knows about the input must pass through mmm, one fixed-size vector, however long the input. Cho et al. (2014) observed that translation quality fell sharply with sentence length. The second problem is §4 again: information about x1x_1x1​ reaches the decoder only after passing through SSS nonlinear recurrent steps.

7.3 Attention

Bahdanau et al. (2015) let the decoder look back at every encoder state, not just the last one. At each decoder step ttt, with decoder state st−1s_{t-1}st−1​:

et,j=score⁡(st−1, hj),αt,j=exp⁡(et,j)∑j′exp⁡(et,j′),mt=∑j=1Sαt,j hj. e_{t,j} = \operatorname{score}\big(s_{t-1},\,h_j\big), \qquad \alpha_{t,j} = \frac{\exp(e_{t,j})}{\sum_{j'}\exp(e_{t,j'})}, \qquad m_t = \sum_{j=1}^{S}\alpha_{t,j}\,h_j . et,j​=score(st−1​,hj​),αt,j​=∑j′​exp(et,j′​)exp(et,j​)​,mt​=j=1∑S​αt,j​hj​.

The weights αt,⋅\alpha_{t,\cdot}αt,⋅​ form a probability distribution over input positions — a soft, learned alignment — and the context mtm_tmt​ is recomputed at every output step. The bottleneck is gone, and every input position is one step away from every output step. In translation, the learned α\alphaα recovered word alignments without supervision.

7.4 Why drop recurrence altogether

Attention was first added on top of recurrent networks. Vaswani et al. (2017) asked what remains if the recurrence is removed and attention is used everywhere, including within the input sequence itself. Compare three ways to build a layer over a sequence of length nnn with width ddd:

7.5 Recurrence is not dead: linear state-space models

A footnote with a future. If the recurrence is linear, ht=Aht−1+Bxth_t = Ah_{t-1}+Bx_tht​=Aht−1​+Bxt​, yt=Chty_t = Ch_tyt​=Cht​ — the state-space model of control theory — then two things change. Stability is controlled directly by the eigenvalues of AAA, which can be parameterized to stay inside the unit circle, so vanishing and exploding gradients can be designed away. And the whole output sequence can be computed in parallel: unrolling gives yt=∑k≥0CAkB xt−ky_t = \sum_{k\ge0}CA^kB\,x_{t-k}yt​=∑k≥0​CAkBxt−k​, a convolution with kernel (CB,CAB,CA2B,… )(CB, CAB, CA^2B,\dots)(CB,CAB,CA2B,…) (Lecture 14), or alternatively a parallel scan with O(log⁡T)O(\log T)O(logT) depth. Structured state-space models (S4; Gu et al., 2022) and their input-dependent, gated successors (Mamba; Gu & Dao, 2023) build deep networks from such layers, interleaved with nonlinearities. They are competitive with transformers on long sequences — and the input-dependent gating that makes them work is the LSTM's idea, returning.

8. Summary (2 min)

An RNN models a sequence through the autoregressive factorization, using a fixed-size state updated by shared weights: a learned discrete dynamical system. Training is BPTT, reverse-mode AD on the unrolled network, with gradients summed over time. The gradient across a lag of T−kT-kT−k steps is a product of T−kT-kT−k Jacobians containing the same WWW, so it vanishes or explodes exponentially, which is a stability property of the learned dynamics (and, for chaotic systems, of the physics itself). LSTMs and GRUs add a gated additive path that acts like a residual connection in time. For forecasting, training on true inputs and testing on predicted ones causes error accumulation, which must be addressed in training and measured in evaluation. Finally, RNNs compute sequentially and connect distant positions through long paths; attention removes both limitations, which takes us to the transformer.
