2. Backpropagation through an MLP (8 min)

Backpropagation is reverse-mode AD applied to the MLP, using the two VJP rules above. Let δ(ℓ)=∂ℓ/∂z(ℓ)\delta^{(\ell)}=\partial\ell/\partial z^{(\ell)}δ(ℓ)=∂ℓ/∂z(ℓ).

  2. Recursion. VJP through W(ℓ+1)W^{(\ell+1)}W(ℓ+1), then through σ\sigmaσ:
δ(ℓ)=σ′(z(ℓ))⊙(W(ℓ+1)⊤δ(ℓ+1)). \delta^{(\ell)} = \sigma'(z^{(\ell)})\odot\big(W^{(\ell+1)\top}\delta^{(\ell+1)}\big). δ(ℓ)=σ′(z(ℓ))⊙(W(ℓ+1)⊤δ(ℓ+1)).

Why depth is dangerous. Unrolling step 2 with D(ℓ)=diag⁡(σ′(z(ℓ)))D^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma'(z^{(\ell)}))D(ℓ)=diag(σ′(z(ℓ))),

δ(ℓ)=D(ℓ)W(ℓ+1)⊤D(ℓ+1)W(ℓ+2)⊤⋯D(L−1)W(L)⊤ δ(L). \delta^{(\ell)} = D^{(\ell)}W^{(\ell+1)\top}D^{(\ell+1)}W^{(\ell+2)\top}\cdots D^{(L-1)}W^{(L)\top}\,\delta^{(L)} . δ(ℓ)=D(ℓ)W(ℓ+1)⊤D(ℓ+1)W(ℓ+2)⊤⋯D(L−1)W(L)⊤δ(L).

A product of L−ℓL-\ellL−ℓ matrices behaves like a product of scalars: unless the typical factor is near one, the gradient shrinks or grows exponentially in depth. The logistic sigmoid has σ′≤1/4\sigma'\le1/4σ′≤1/4, so across 10 layers the first-layer gradient can be smaller by ∼4−9≈4×10−6\sim4^{-9}\approx4\times10^{-6}∼4−9≈4×10−6 — the vanishing-gradient problem that stalled the second wave. The forward pass has the same structure, so activations can vanish or explode too. §§3–4 choose scales that keep both products near one.

2. Automatic differentiation: JVP, VJP, grad (20 min)

Three ways to get a derivative. Symbolic differentiation produces exact expressions that grow uncontrollably for long programs. Finite differences need one extra evaluation per input — 10610^6106 evaluations for 10610^6106 parameters — and trade truncation error against roundoff. Automatic differentiation (AD) gives derivatives exact to machine precision at a cost that is a small multiple of evaluating the function. It does this by applying the chain rule to the program, one primitive operation at a time.

A program is a composition. Write f=fK∘⋯∘f1f = f_K\circ\cdots\circ f_1f=fK​∘⋯∘f1​ with x0=xx_0=xx0​=x and xk=fk(xk−1)x_k = f_k(x_{k-1})xk​=fk​(xk−1​). By the chain rule,

Jf(x)=JK JK−1⋯J1,Jk=∂fk∂xk−1. J_f(x) = J_K\,J_{K-1}\cdots J_1, \qquad J_k = \frac{\partial f_k}{\partial x_{k-1}}. Jf​(x)=JK​JK−1​⋯J1​,Jk​=∂xk−1​∂fk​​.

AD never forms JfJ_fJf​. It only multiplies by it, from one side or the other.

Forward mode: the Jacobian–vector product (JVP). Given a tangent direction v∈Rnv\in\mathbb{R}^nv∈Rn, compute Jfv=JK(⋯(J2(J1v)))J_f v = J_K(\cdots(J_2(J_1v)))Jf​v=JK​(⋯(J2​(J1​v))), right to left. Carry a tangent x˙k\dot x_kx˙k​ alongside each value:

xk=fk(xk−1),x˙k=Jk x˙k−1,x˙0=v. x_k = f_k(x_{k-1}), \qquad \dot x_k = J_k\,\dot x_{k-1}, \qquad \dot x_0 = v. xk​=fk​(xk−1​),x˙k​=Jk​x˙k−1​,x˙0​=v.

One forward pass gives f(x)f(x)f(x) and the directional derivative JfvJ_fvJf​v, at a cost of a small constant times evaluating fff, with no extra memory. A full Jacobian takes nnn passes, one per input direction.

Reverse mode: the vector–Jacobian product (VJP). Given a cotangent u∈Rmu\in\mathbb{R}^mu∈Rm, compute u⊤Jf=((u⊤JK)JK−1)⋯J1u^\top J_f = ((u^\top J_K)J_{K-1})\cdots J_1u⊤Jf​=((u⊤JK​)JK−1​)⋯J1​, left to right. Run the program forward, storing every xkx_kxk​, then sweep backward:

xˉK=u,xˉk−1=Jk⊤ xˉk. \bar x_K = u, \qquad \bar x_{k-1} = J_k^\top\,\bar x_k . xˉK​=u,xˉk−1​=Jk⊤​xˉk​.

One forward-plus-backward pass gives u⊤Jfu^\top J_fu⊤Jf​, again at a small constant times the cost of fff, but with memory proportional to the number of stored intermediates. A full Jacobian takes mmm passes, one per output.

grad is one VJP. A training loss is a scalar, L:Rn→R\mathcal L:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}L:Rn→R, so m=1m=1m=1 and

∇L(θ)⊤=1⋅JL(θ). \nabla\mathcal L(\theta)^\top = 1\cdot J_{\mathcal L}(\theta). ∇L(θ)⊤=1⋅JL​(θ).

One reverse pass gives the entire gradient, whatever the number of parameters. Forward mode would need nnn passes. This asymmetry is why every deep learning framework is built on reverse mode, and why training is limited by activation memory rather than arithmetic.

Remember. JVP pushes a tangent forward; VJP pulls a cotangent back. A gradient is one VJP, so it costs a small multiple of one function evaluation regardless of the number of parameters — paid for in memory.

3. Data normalization (5 min)

Standardize every input feature with its training-set mean and standard deviation, and apply the same transformation to validation and test data. For regression, standardize the targets too and invert at prediction time.

  • Conditioning. The first-layer gradient is δ(1)x⊤\delta^{(1)}x^\topδ(1)x⊤, so the first layer sees its inputs exactly as linear regression does: mismatched scales mean a large condition number (Lecture 6).
  • Centering. If every xj>0x_j>0xj​>0, all components of a unit's weight gradient δixj\delta_ix_jδi​xj​ share the sign of δi\delta_iδi​, so the weights can only move together, and the optimizer zig-zags. The same argument condemns always-positive hidden activations such as the logistic sigmoid.
  • Signal scale. The initialization analysis of §4 assumes unit-variance inputs. A feature with standard deviation 1000 saturates tanh units at once, and tanh⁡′(10)≈8×10−9\tanh'(10)\approx8\times10^{-9}tanh′(10)≈8×10−9.

For physical data, nondimensionalize first, then standardize; log-transform strongly skewed positive quantities.

Normalizing inside the network. Input normalization and initialization fix the scale at step 0 only. As the weights train, hidden activations drift, and the analysis of §4 no longer holds. Two architectural ingredients keep deep networks trainable throughout: normalization layers fix the forward scale at every step, and residual connections fix the backward product of §2.

Layer normalization (Ba et al., 2016) re-standardizes each example's hidden vector h∈RHh\in\mathbb{R}^Hh∈RH:

LN⁡(h)=γ⊙h−μs2+ϵ+β,μ=1H∑j=1Hhj,s2=1H∑j=1H(hj−μ)2. \operatorname{LN}(h)=\gamma\odot\frac{h-\mu}{\sqrt{s^2+\epsilon}}+\beta,\qquad \mu=\frac1H\sum_{j=1}^H h_j,\quad s^2=\frac1H\sum_{j=1}^H(h_j-\mu)^2 . LN(h)=γ⊙s2+ϵ​h−μ​+β,μ=H1​j=1∑H​hj​,s2=H1​j=1∑H​(hj​−μ)2.

The statistics are taken across the units of one example, so LN behaves identically at training and test time and for any batch size. The learned gain γ\gammaγ and shift β\betaβ let the network undo the normalization where that helps.

Batch normalization (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) takes the same statistics across the examples of a minibatch instead. It works well for convolutional networks with large batches, but it couples the examples in a batch, degrades at small batch sizes, and must switch to stored running averages at test time — a common source of train/test discrepancies. LayerNorm is the standard choice in transformers and most scientific ML models.

Residual connections (He et al., 2016) attack the vanishing gradient directly. Instead of h(ℓ)=Fℓ(h(ℓ−1))h^{(\ell)}=F_\ell(h^{(\ell-1)})h(ℓ)=Fℓ​(h(ℓ−1)), a residual block computes

h(ℓ)=h(ℓ−1)+Fℓ(h(ℓ−1)), h^{(\ell)} = h^{(\ell-1)} + F_\ell\big(h^{(\ell-1)}\big), h(ℓ)=h(ℓ−1)+Fℓ​(h(ℓ−1)),

so each layer learns a correction to its input rather than a whole new representation. Now look at backpropagation. In §2 the error reaching layer ℓ\ellℓ was a product of Jacobians D(k)W(k+1)⊤D^{(k)}W^{(k+1)\top}D(k)W(k+1)⊤, and any product of many factors shrinks or grows exponentially. A residual block has Jacobian I+JFℓI+J_{F_\ell}I+JFℓ​​, so its VJP is

hˉ(ℓ−1)=hˉ(ℓ)+JFℓ⊤ hˉ(ℓ), \bar h^{(\ell-1)} = \bar h^{(\ell)} + J_{F_\ell}^\top\,\bar h^{(\ell)}, hˉ(ℓ−1)=hˉ(ℓ)+JFℓ​⊤​hˉ(ℓ),

and unrolling over the remaining layers gives

hˉ(ℓ)=∏k=ℓ+1L(I+JFk⊤) hˉ(L)=hˉ(L)+∑kJFk⊤hˉ(L)+⋯ . \bar h^{(\ell)} = \prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}\big(I+J_{F_k}^\top\big)\,\bar h^{(L)} = \bar h^{(L)} + \sum_{k}J_{F_k}^\top\bar h^{(L)} + \cdots . hˉ(ℓ)=k=ℓ+1∏L​(I+JFk​⊤​)hˉ(L)=hˉ(L)+k∑​JFk​⊤​hˉ(L)+⋯.

The first term is the output error delivered to layer ℓ\ellℓ unchanged, through the chain of identity paths. However deep the network and however small the JFkJ_{F_k}JFk​​, that term cannot vanish multiplicatively: the gradient has a highway around every block. If the blocks start small — near-zero FkF_kFk​ at initialization — every factor is close to III and the product neither vanishes nor explodes. This is why residual networks train at depths of hundreds of layers where plain MLPs of the kind in §2 fail at a few dozen. The one constraint is that FℓF_\ellFℓ​ must preserve the dimension, so the identity can be added.

The two combined. Modern deep architectures, transformers included, stack blocks of the form

h(ℓ)=h(ℓ−1)+Fℓ(LN⁡(h(ℓ−1))), h^{(\ell)} = h^{(\ell-1)} + F_\ell\big(\operatorname{LN}(h^{(\ell-1)})\big), h(ℓ)=h(ℓ−1)+Fℓ​(LN(h(ℓ−1))),

with the normalization inside the branch and the identity path left untouched. LN keeps each block's input at unit scale; the residual keeps the gradient path open. For the shallow MLPs of this lecture both are optional. For anything deep, this block is the default.

Remember. Initialization fixes the scale at step 0. LayerNorm restores unit scale in the forward pass at every step. Residual connections replace the backprop product ∏D W⊤\prod D\,W^\top∏DW⊤ by ∏(I+JF⊤)\prod(I+J_F^\top)∏(I+JF⊤​), which always contains an identity path, so gradients reach the early layers intact.

4. Initialization (17 min)

Not zero. Units that start identical receive identical gradients and stay identical forever. Weights must be random; biases can start at zero.

With what variance? In a width-nnn linear network with weight variance vvv, each layer multiplies the signal variance by nvnvnv, so LLL layers multiply it by (nv)L(nv)^L(nv)L. Only nv≈1nv\approx1nv≈1 is stable. Make this precise.

Assumptions at initialization: zero biases; i.i.d. zero-mean weights with variance vℓv_\ellvℓ​, independent of the layer input.

Forward.

  1. zi(ℓ)=∑j=1ninWijhjz^{(\ell)}_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{in}}}W_{ij}h_jzi(ℓ)​=∑j=1nin​​Wij​hj​ is a sum of independent zero-mean terms, each with variance E[Wij2]E[hj2]=vℓ E[hj2]\mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^2]\mathbb{E}[h_j^2]=v_\ell\,\mathbb{E}[h_j^2]E[Wij2​]E[hj2​]=vℓ​E[hj2​].

What they observed. The earlier "standard" initialization U[−1/nin,1/nin]\mathcal U[-1/\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}},1/\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}}]U[−1/nin​​,1/nin​​] has variance 1/(3nin)1/(3n_{\text{in}})1/(3nin​), so nv=1/3nv=1/3nv=1/3: the signal variance falls by a factor of 3 per layer, and in their tanh networks the layers saturated one after another. With the logistic sigmoid, which is not zero-centered and has σ′(0)=1/4\sigma'(0)=1/4σ′(0)=1/4, the top hidden layer saturated at once and stayed stuck. With normalized initialization, activation and gradient statistics stayed similar across layers and training was faster. They also found cross-entropy far better behaved than squared error for classification — the Lecture 5 lesson again.

vℓ=2nin. v_\ell=\frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}. vℓ​=nin​2​.

Remember. Variance 1/nin1/n_{\text{in}}1/nin​ preserves the forward signal for tanh, 2/nin2/n_{\text{in}}2/nin​ for ReLU; Glorot averages the forward and backward conditions. 5. Optimizers (12 min)

Everything in Lecture 6 still applies: the stability threshold explains divergence, Adam's diagonal preconditioner handles the very different gradient scales of different layers, and the SGD noise floor is why schedules end with a decay. What is lost is convexity. In practice, large networks usually reach low training loss; the obstacles are plateaus and bad scaling more often than bad local minima. That is an empirical observation, not a theorem.

Tune the learning rate first. Sweep it on a log grid (10−410^{-4}10−4 to 10−210^{-2}10−2) before touching anything else. The best value is usually just below where training goes unstable.

AdamW, not Adam with L2.

  1. Adding λ2∥θ∥2\frac\lambda2\lVert\theta\rVert^22λ​∥θ∥2 to the loss adds λθ\lambda\thetaλθ to the gradient.
  2. Under SGD, θ←(1−ηλ)θ−ηg\theta\leftarrow(1-\eta\lambda)\theta-\eta gθ←(1−ηλ)θ−ηg: every weight shrinks by the same factor. L2 regularization is weight decay.
  3. Under Adam, λθ\lambda\thetaλθ is divided by v^\sqrt{\hat v}v^​, so weights with large gradient history are decayed less and others more: no longer uniform, no longer a fixed Gaussian prior.
  4. AdamW applies the decay outside the preconditioner:

6. Regularization as priors (18 min)

Seeing overfitting requires three splits. Training data compute gradients; validation data make every decision (learning rate, decay, architecture, when to stop); test data are used once, to report. A test set that influenced any decision is a second validation set, and its number is optimistic. The symptom is a widening gap between training and validation loss.

Each standard regularizer has a probabilistic reading.

Weight decay is MAP. With prior θ∼N(0,τ2I)\theta\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)θ∼N(0,τ2I), the negative log posterior divided by NNN is L(θ)+λ2∥θ∥2\mathcal L(\theta)+\frac\lambda2\lVert\theta\rVert^2L(θ)+2λ​∥θ∥2 with λ=1/(Nτ2)\lambda=1/(N\tau^2)λ=1/(Nτ2) — Lecture 5's ridge derivation, which never used linearity. Exact under SGD; only approximate under AdamW (§5). Biases are usually not decayed.

Dropout is noise injection. During training, zero each hidden unit with probability ppp and rescale the survivors: h~j=mjhj/(1−p)\tilde h_j=m_jh_j/(1-p)h~j​=mj​hj​/(1−p), mj∼Ber⁡(1−p)m_j\sim\operatorname{Ber}(1-p)mj​∼Ber(1−p). a ridge penalty weighted by feature energy. In deep networks it penalizes reliance on any single unit. Typical p∈[0.1,0.5]p\in[0.1,0.5]p∈[0.1,0.5]; most useful when data are scarce.

Data augmentation encodes an invariance. Adding transformed copies of the training data tells the model the label is unchanged by the transformation. The invariance must actually hold: a rotated "6" is a "9," and rotating a velocity field requires rotating its vectors too. An augmentation that breaks the true symmetry is label noise, not regularization.

Remember. Weight decay = Gaussian prior; early stopping ≈ ridge with α≈1/(ηk)\alpha\approx1/(\eta k)α≈1/(ηk); dropout = multiplicative noise (ridge for linear models); augmentation = an invariance assumption. Decide everything on validation data; touch the test set once.

7. Overparameterization and implicit bias (4 min)

Networks often have more parameters than data and still generalize. Part of the reason is that the optimizer chooses among the many solutions that fit the data. The simplest case: least squares with Φ∈RN×p\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}Φ∈RN×p, p>Np>Np>N, run from θ0=0\theta_0=0θ0​=0.

  1. The gradient Φ⊤(Φθ−y)\Phi^\top(\Phi\theta-y)Φ⊤(Φθ−y) lies in the row space of Φ\PhiΦ, so every iterate does too.
  2. Every interpolant is θmn+n\theta_{\text{mn}}+nθmn​+n with nnn in the null space, orthogonal to the row space, so the only interpolant in the row space is the minimum-norm one, Φ+y\Phi^+yΦ+y.

Gradient descent returns the minimum-norm interpolant without being asked to; the optimizer and the initialization are part of the model. This also explains double descent: test error peaks near p≈Np\approx Np≈N, where the smallest singular value of Φ\PhiΦ approaches zero and noise is amplified by 1/smin⁡1/s_{\min}1/smin​ (Lecture 4). Past that point it falls again, and a small ridge penalty removes the peak.

In practice: if a large network overfits, try more data, augmentation, decay and early stopping before shrinking it. With only a few hundred samples, compare against a GP — you may be in the classical regime.

8. Training checklist (6 min)

Before training.

  1. Fit a baseline on the same inputs — logistic regression for classification (Coding Assignment 1), a GP for small regression problems.
  2. Check the loss at step 0: about log⁡C\log ClogC for CCC-class cross-entropy (2.302.302.30 for C=10C=10C=10), order one for standardized regression targets. Anything else is a scaling or label bug.
  3. Overfit one batch of 32 examples to near-zero loss. If you can't, there is a bug.

During and after training.

  1. Plot training and validation loss together; change one thing at a time.
  2. Run at least 5 seeds and report mean ±\pm± std. Differences smaller than the seed spread are not results.

Quiz-eligible facts

  1. A JVP costs one forward pass and gives JfvJ_fvJf​v; a VJP costs one forward-plus-backward pass and gives u⊤Jfu^\top J_fu⊤Jf​. A full Jacobian needs nnn JVPs or mmm VJPs.
  2. The gradient of a scalar loss is one VJP with u=1u=1u=1, so it costs a small multiple of one function evaluation regardless of the number of parameters; reverse mode pays for this in memory.
  3. Forward-over-reverse gives Hessian–vector products without forming the Hessian.
  4. Backprop: δ(ℓ)=σ′(z(ℓ))⊙W(ℓ+1)⊤δ(ℓ+1)\delta^{(\ell)}=\sigma'(z^{(\ell)})\odot W^{(\ell+1)\top}\delta^{(\ell+1)}δ(ℓ)=σ′(z(ℓ))⊙W(ℓ+1)⊤δ(ℓ+1) and ∂ℓ/∂W(ℓ)=δ(ℓ)h(ℓ−1)⊤\partial\ell/\partial W^{(\ell)}=\delta^{(\ell)}h^{(\ell-1)\top}∂ℓ/∂W(ℓ)=δ(ℓ)h(ℓ−1)⊤; gradient size scales exponentially with depth unless each factor is near one.
  5. Inputs are standardized with training statistics only; all-positive inputs force a unit's weight gradients to share a sign.
  6. Initialization variances: 1/nin1/n_{\text{in}}1/nin​ (forward, tanh), 2/(nin+nout)2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})2/(nin​+nout​) (Glorot), 2/nin2/n_{\text{in}}2/nin​ (He, ReLU).   7. Under SGD, L2 regularization equals weight decay and is MAP with τ2=1/(Nλ)\tau^2=1/(N\lambda)τ2=1/(Nλ); under Adam it does not, and AdamW decouples the decay.
  8. Early stopping on a quadratic is approximately ridge with α≈1/(ηk)\alpha\approx1/(\eta k)α≈1/(ηk).
