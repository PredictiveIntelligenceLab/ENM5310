1. Why multi-fidelity: the budget problem

The situation is completely standard in engineering. You have two ways to evaluate the same quantity:

Cost per run Accuracy Runs you can afford
Low fidelity minutes biased, but captures the trends thousands
High fidelity hours or days what you actually want tens

A coarse mesh versus a fine one; RANS versus LES; a two-dimensional idealization versus the full three-dimensional geometry; a surrogate physics model versus an experiment. The pattern is always the same: NL≫NHN_L \gg N_HNL​≫NH​, and it is fHf_HfH​ you care about.

Two obvious strategies, both bad. Fit a GP to the high-fidelity data alone and you have a handful of points in a possibly multi-dimensional space — Lecture 9 §3.3 tells you exactly what happens, namely a posterior that reverts to the prior almost everywhere. Fit a GP to the low-fidelity data alone and you get a beautiful, confident surrogate for the wrong function.

The multi-fidelity idea is to model both functions jointly, so that the cheap data constrains the expensive function through whatever correlation exists between them. The engineering intuition that makes it work:

You do not have to learn the expensive function from scratch. Learn its shape cheaply, then use the few expensive runs to learn only the correction.

If the correction is simpler than the function — smoother, smaller, slowly varying — then a handful of high-fidelity points is enough to pin it down. That "if" is a modeling assumption, it is testable, and §8 shows how to test it.

2. Multi-output GPs: a GP on an augmented input space

Before specializing to fidelities, do the general case: TTT related functions f1,…,fTf_1,\dots,f_Tf1​,…,fT​ on the same input space, modeled jointly.

The move that makes this easy. Instead of thinking of a vector-valued function f(x)∈RT\mathbf{f}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^Tf(x)∈RT, think of a scalar function on a bigger input space:

f~: X×{1,…,T} → R,f~(x,t)≜ft(x).\tilde f:\ \mathcal{X}\times\{1,\dots,T\}\ \to\ \mathbb{R}, \qquad \tilde f(x,t) \triangleq f_t(x).f~​: X×{1,…,T} → R,f~​(x,t)≜ft​(x).

An input is now a pair: where, and which function. A GP on this augmented space needs a kernel taking two such pairs,

 k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Cov⁡[ft(x), ft′(x′)], \boxed{\ k\big((x,t),(x',t')\big) = \operatorname{Cov}\big[f_t(x),\,f_{t'}(x')\big],\ } k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Cov[ft​(x),ft′​(x′)], ​

which must be positive semi-definite in the usual sense — every Gram matrix built from any finite set of (input, label) pairs must be a valid covariance matrix. Nothing else changes.

Why this is worth stating explicitly. Every formula from Lecture 9 now applies verbatim, with no new derivations:

  • Training is still log⁡p(y∣θ)=−12y⊤C−1y−12log⁡∣C∣−N2log⁡2π\log p(y\mid\theta) = -\frac12y^\top C^{-1}y-\frac12\log|C|-\frac N2\log2\pilogp(y∣θ)=−21​y⊤C−1y−21​log∣C∣−2N​log2π, where yyy stacks all observations from all outputs and CCC is built from the augmented kernel plus per-output noise.
  • Inference is still conditioning: to predict ft(x⋆)f_t(x^\star)ft​(x⋆), form k⋆k^\stark⋆ with entries k((xn,tn),(x⋆,t))k((x_n,t_n),(x^\star,t))k((xn​,tn​),(x⋆,t)) and apply μ⋆=k⋆⊤C−1y\mu^\star = k^{\star\top}C^{-1}yμ⋆=k⋆⊤C−1y, v⋆=k⋆⋆−k⋆⊤C−1k⋆v^\star = k^{\star\star}-k^{\star\top}C^{-1}k^\starv⋆=k⋆⋆−k⋆⊤C−1k⋆.
  • Computation is still one Cholesky factorization, now of an N×NN\times NN×N matrix with N=∑tNtN = \sum_t N_tN=∑t​Nt​.

The only new modeling question is what the cross-covariances k((x,t),(x′,t′))k((x,t),(x',t'))k((x,t),(x′,t′)) for t≠t′t\ne t't=t′ should be. That is the whole subject.

Block structure. Order the stacked data by output. Then CCC has T×TT\times TT×T blocks, the (t,t′)(t,t')(t,t′) block being the Nt×Nt′N_t\times N_{t'}Nt​×Nt′​ matrix of cross-covariances between output ttt at its inputs and output t′t't′ at its inputs:

C  =  (K11(X1,X1)+σ12I⋯K1T(X1,XT)⋮⋱⋮KT1(XT,X1)⋯KTT(XT,XT)+σT2I).C \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} K_{11}(X_1,X_1)+\sigma_1^2 I & \cdots & K_{1T}(X_1,X_T)\\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ K_{T1}(X_T,X_1) & \cdots & K_{TT}(X_T,X_T)+\sigma_T^2 I\end{pmatrix}.C=⎝⎛​K11​(X1​,X1​)+σ12​I⋮KT1​(XT​,X1​)​⋯⋱⋯​K1T​(X1​,XT​)⋮KTT​(XT​,XT​)+σT2​I​⎠⎞​.

The diagonal blocks are ordinary single-output GPs. The off-diagonal blocks are the entire point: they are what lets an observation of output t′t't′ change the posterior for output ttt. Set them to zero and the whole thing decouples into TTT independent GPs, which is exactly the "fit them separately" strategy §1 rejected.

Note the inputs need not be shared: output ttt is observed at XtX_tXt​, and the sets may be different sizes and disjoint. This matters immediately, because low- and high-fidelity runs are rarely at the same design points.

3. Separable covariance, and its limitation

The simplest valid construction takes one ordinary kernel kkk on X\mathcal{X}X and one T×TT\times TT×T matrix BBB describing how the outputs relate, and multiplies them:

Intrinsic coregionalization model (ICM).

k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Btt′  k(x,x′),B⪰0.k\big((x,t),(x',t')\big) = B_{tt'}\;k(x,x'), \qquad B\succeq0 .k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Btt′​k(x,x′),B⪰0.

BBB is the coregionalization matrix: BttB_{tt}Btt​ is the variance of output ttt, and Btt′B_{tt'}Btt′​ its covariance with output t′t't′.

Validity is easy to see. Write B=WW⊤B = WW^\topB=WW⊤ (always possible for B⪰0B\succeq0B⪰0), with wtw_twt​ the ttt-th row of WWW. Then Btt′=wt⊤wt′B_{tt'} = w_t^\top w_{t'}Btt′​=wt⊤​wt′​, and the model is exactly

ft(x)=∑q=1QWtq uq(x),uq∼GP(0,k)  independent.f_t(x) = \sum_{q=1}^{Q} W_{tq}\,u_q(x), \qquad u_q\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)\ \text{ independent}.ft​(x)=q=1∑Q​Wtq​uq​(x),uq​∼GP(0,k)  independent.

Every output is a different linear combination of the same QQQ shared latent functions. That is where the correlation comes from, and it is why BBB must be positive semi-definite: it is a Gram matrix of the mixing weights.

Allowing each latent process its own kernel gives the general form:

Linear model of coregionalization (LMC).

k((x,t),(x′,t′))=∑q=1QBtt′(q)  kq(x,x′),B(q)=w(q)(w(q))⊤.k\big((x,t),(x',t')\big) = \sum_{q=1}^{Q} B^{(q)}_{tt'}\;k_q(x,x'), \qquad B^{(q)} = w^{(q)}\big(w^{(q)}\big)^\top .k((x,t),(x′,t′))=q=1∑Q​Btt′(q)​kq​(x,x′),B(q)=w(q)(w(q))⊤.

What ICM cannot do, and why it matters here. With a single shared kkk, all outputs have the same length scale and the same smoothness, and the correlation between outputs is the same constant everywhere in input space. For multi-fidelity that is too rigid: a high-fidelity simulation typically has more fine structure than its coarse counterpart, and the two often agree well in some regions and poorly in others. The next section's model is an LMC with Q=2Q = 2Q=2 and a specific choice of weights, designed precisely to relax this.

4. Step 1 — Model: the autoregressive multi-fidelity GP

4.1 The model

Kennedy and O'Hagan's proposal — also called co-kriging — is one line:

 fH(x)  =  ρ fL(x)  +  δ(x),fL∼GP(0,kL),δ∼GP(0,kδ),fL⊥δ. \boxed{\ f_H(x) \;=\; \rho\,f_L(x)\;+\;\delta(x), \qquad f_L\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k_L),\quad \delta\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k_\delta),\quad f_L\perp\delta .\ } fH​(x)=ρfL​(x)+δ(x),fL​∼GP(0,kL​),δ∼GP(0,kδ​),fL​⊥δ. ​

In words: the expensive function is a rescaled copy of the cheap one, plus an independent correction. The scalar ρ\rhoρ absorbs a systematic difference in magnitude; the discrepancy δ\deltaδ absorbs everything else, and carries its own kernel with its own amplitude and length scale.

Observations at the two levels are noisy:

yL=fL(XL)+ϵL,ϵL∼N(0,σL2I),yH=fH(XH)+ϵH,ϵH∼N(0,σH2I).y_L = f_L(X_L)+\epsilon_L,\quad \epsilon_L\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_L^2 I), \qquad y_H = f_H(X_H)+\epsilon_H,\quad \epsilon_H\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_H^2 I).yL​=fL​(XL​)+ϵL​,ϵL​∼N(0,σL2​I),yH​=fH​(XH​)+ϵH​,ϵH​∼N(0,σH2​I).

For a deterministic simulator, take the corresponding σt2→0\sigma_t^2\to0σt2​→0 (Lecture 9 §4.4) and expect to need jitter.

4.2 The three covariance functions

Everything follows from bilinearity of covariance and the independence of fLf_LfL​ and δ\deltaδ:

Cov⁡[fL(x),fL(x′)]=kL(x,x′),\operatorname{Cov}\big[f_L(x),f_L(x')\big] = k_L(x,x'),Cov[fL​(x),fL​(x′)]=kL​(x,x′),
Cov⁡[fH(x),fL(x′)]=Cov⁡[ρfL(x)+δ(x), fL(x′)]=ρ kL(x,x′),\operatorname{Cov}\big[f_H(x),f_L(x')\big] = \operatorname{Cov}\big[\rho f_L(x)+\delta(x),\,f_L(x')\big] = \rho\,k_L(x,x'),Cov[fH​(x),fL​(x′)]=Cov[ρfL​(x)+δ(x),fL​(x′)]=ρkL​(x,x′),
Cov⁡[fH(x),fH(x′)]=ρ2kL(x,x′)+kδ(x,x′).\operatorname{Cov}\big[f_H(x),f_H(x')\big] = \rho^2 k_L(x,x') + k_\delta(x,x').Cov[fH​(x),fH​(x′)]=ρ2kL​(x,x′)+kδ​(x,x′).

Three readings worth pausing on. The cross-covariance is ρkL\rho k_LρkL​, so ρ\rhoρ is the entire channel through which the cheap data speaks about the expensive function — at ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0 the off-diagonal blocks vanish and the two models decouple. The high-fidelity kernel ρ2kL+kδ\rho^2k_L+k_\deltaρ2kL​+kδ​ is a sum, so by Lecture 9 §4.1 it inherits structure from both: the coarse shape from kLk_LkL​ and any additional fine structure from kδk_\deltakδ​. And the ICM restriction of §3 is gone, since the two terms may have different length scales.

4.3 It is an LMC with two latent processes

Collect the covariances above. With the label set {L,H}\{L,H\}{L,H},

k((x,t),(x′,t′))=at at′ kL(x,x′)  +  bt bt′ kδ(x,x′),a=(1ρ),b=(01),k\big((x,t),(x',t')\big) = a_t\,a_{t'}\,k_L(x,x') \;+\; b_t\,b_{t'}\,k_\delta(x,x'), \qquad a = \begin{pmatrix}1\\\rho\end{pmatrix},\quad b = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},k((x,t),(x′,t′))=at​at′​kL​(x,x′)+bt​bt′​kδ​(x,x′),a=(1ρ​),b=(01​),

which is exactly the LMC of §3 with Q=2Q = 2Q=2, latent processes u1=fLu_1 = f_Lu1​=fL​ and u2=δu_2 = \deltau2​=δ, and coregionalization matrices

B(1)=aa⊤=(1ρρρ2),B(2)=bb⊤=(0001).B^{(1)} = aa^\top = \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\ \rho&\rho^2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B^{(2)} = bb^\top = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.B(1)=aa⊤=(1ρ​ρρ2​),B(2)=bb⊤=(00​01​).

So the multi-fidelity model is a multi-output GP whose coregionalization structure has been specialized in two ways: the first latent process is shared with weights (1,ρ)(1,\rho)(1,ρ), and the second is exclusive to the high-fidelity output. The rank-one B(1)B^{(1)}B(1) is the assumption that one shared latent function explains all the commonality; the triangular pattern of B(2)B^{(2)}B(2) is the assumption that fidelities are ordered — the correction belongs to the expensive level only. Neither is forced on you by the mathematics; both encode real beliefs about the physics.

4.4 More than two levels

For TTT ordered fidelities, apply the same relation recursively:

ft(x)=ρt−1ft−1(x)+δt(x),t=2,…,T,f_t(x) = \rho_{t-1}f_{t-1}(x) + \delta_t(x), \qquad t = 2,\dots,T,ft​(x)=ρt−1​ft−1​(x)+δt​(x),t=2,…,T,

with all δt\delta_tδt​ independent of each other and of f1f_1f1​. Each level has its own scaling and its own discrepancy kernel. The Markov property implicit here is worth naming: given ft−1(x)f_{t-1}(x)ft−1​(x), no other value ft−1(x′)f_{t-1}(x')ft−1​(x′) tells you anything more about ft(x)f_t(x)ft​(x). All the information passes through the same input location. Section 5 turns that into a training algorithm.

5. Step 2 — Training

5.1 The joint approach

The hyperparameters are θ=(θL, θδ, ρ, σL2, σH2)\theta = (\theta_L,\ \theta_\delta,\ \rho,\ \sigma_L^2,\ \sigma_H^2)θ=(θL​, θδ​, ρ, σL2​, σH2​). Stack the data and build the block covariance from §4.2:

y=(yLyH),Cθ=(kL(XL,XL)+σL2Iρ kL(XL,XH)ρ kL(XH,XL)ρ2kL(XH,XH)+kδ(XH,XH)+σH2I).y = \begin{pmatrix}y_L\\y_H\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} k_L(X_L,X_L)+\sigma_L^2I & \rho\,k_L(X_L,X_H)\\[2pt] \rho\,k_L(X_H,X_L) & \rho^2k_L(X_H,X_H)+k_\delta(X_H,X_H)+\sigma_H^2I \end{pmatrix}.y=(yL​yH​​),Cθ​=(kL​(XL​,XL​)+σL2​IρkL​(XH​,XL​)​ρkL​(XL​,XH​)ρ2kL​(XH​,XH​)+kδ​(XH​,XH​)+σH2​I​).

Then train exactly as in Lecture 9 §2 — nothing is new:

θ^=arg⁡max⁡θ [−12y⊤Cθ−1y−12log⁡∣Cθ∣−N2log⁡2π],\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta\ \left[-\tfrac12y^\top C_\theta^{-1}y-\tfrac12\log|C_\theta|-\tfrac N2\log2\pi\right],θ^=argθmax​ [−21​y⊤Cθ−1​y−21​log∣Cθ​∣−2N​log2π],

by L-BFGS on the log-hyperparameters, with ρ\rhoρ left unconstrained since it may be negative. Store the Cholesky factor LLL of Cθ^C_{\hat\theta}Cθ^​ and α=Cθ^−1y\alpha = C_{\hat\theta}^{-1}yα=Cθ^−1​y.

The cost is the problem. CCC is (NL+NH)×(NL+NH)(N_L+N_H)\times(N_L+N_H)(NL​+NH​)×(NL​+NH​), so the factorization is O((NL+NH)3)O\big((N_L+N_H)^3\big)O((NL​+NH​)3) — and NLN_LNL​ is large by construction, which is the whole reason the low-fidelity model was attractive. The joint fit also couples all the hyperparameters into one non-convex optimization, making the local optima of Lecture 9 §2.7 considerably worse.

5.2 The recursive approach

The Markov structure of §4.4 suggests fitting one level at a time.

Algorithm: recursive multi-fidelity training (two levels)

  1. Fit the low fidelity alone. Train an ordinary GP on (XL,yL)(X_L,y_L)(XL​,yL​) for (θL,σL2)(\theta_L,\sigma_L^2)(θL​,σL2​), exactly as in Lecture 9. This gives a posterior fL∣yL∼GP(μL,vL)f_L\mid y_L\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_L,v_L)fL​∣yL​∼GP(μL​,vL​).
  2. Fit the discrepancy. Train an ordinary GP on the residuals
(XH,  yH−ρ μL(XH))\big(X_H,\ \ y_H-\rho\,\mu_L(X_H)\big)(XH​,  yH​−ρμL​(XH​))

for (θδ,ρ,σH2)(\theta_\delta,\rho,\sigma_H^2)(θδ​,ρ,σH2​), treating ρ\rhoρ as one more hyperparameter of that second fit.

Two fits of ordinary GPs, at a cost of O(NL3)+O(NH3)O(N_L^3)+O(N_H^3)O(NL3​)+O(NH3​) instead of O((NL+NH)3)O\big((N_L+N_H)^3\big)O((NL​+NH​)3), with two small optimizations instead of one large one. Le Gratiet and Garnier showed that when the designs are nested, XH⊆XLX_H\subseteq X_LXH​⊆XL​, the recursive scheme reproduces the joint model exactly rather than approximating it.

This is a strong argument for choosing nested designs when you control the experiment: evaluate the expensive model at a subset of the points where you evaluated the cheap one. The result is cheaper to fit, better conditioned as an optimization problem, and identical in what it predicts.

One caution. Step 2 uses the posterior mean μL(XH)\mu_L(X_H)μL​(XH​) as if it were the truth. Doing this properly requires propagating vLv_LvL​ into the second stage; with a nested design and dense low-fidelity data the difference is small, which is why the simple version is used in practice.

6. Step 3 — Inference, and what the cheap data bought you

To predict the high-fidelity function at x⋆x^\starx⋆, condition as always. The cross-covariance vector between fH(x⋆)f_H(x^\star)fH​(x⋆) and the stacked data follows from §4.2:

k⋆=(ρ kL(XL,x⋆)ρ2kL(XH,x⋆)+kδ(XH,x⋆)),k⋆⋆=ρ2kL(x⋆,x⋆)+kδ(x⋆,x⋆),k^\star = \begin{pmatrix}\rho\,k_L(X_L,x^\star)\\[2pt] \rho^2k_L(X_H,x^\star)+k_\delta(X_H,x^\star)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad k^{\star\star} = \rho^2k_L(x^\star,x^\star)+k_\delta(x^\star,x^\star),k⋆=(ρkL​(XL​,x⋆)ρ2kL​(XH​,x⋆)+kδ​(XH​,x⋆)​),k⋆⋆=ρ2kL​(x⋆,x⋆)+kδ​(x⋆,x⋆),

and then, with LLL and α\alphaα from training,

 μH⋆=k⋆⊤α,vH⋆=k⋆⋆−∥L−1k⋆∥2. \boxed{\ \mu_H^\star = k^{\star\top}\alpha, \qquad v_H^\star = k^{\star\star}-\big\|L^{-1}k^\star\big\|^2 .\ } μH⋆​=k⋆⊤α,vH⋆​=k⋆⋆−∥∥​L−1k⋆∥∥​2. ​

Identical in form to Lecture 9 §3. Only the kernel changed.

Where the gain comes from. Look at the top block of k⋆k^\stark⋆: it is ρ kL(XL,x⋆)\rho\,k_L(X_L,x^\star)ρkL​(XL​,x⋆), nonzero wherever low-fidelity data sits near x⋆x^\starx⋆. Every cheap run therefore reduces vH⋆v_H^\starvH⋆​, in proportion to ρ\rhoρ. The high-fidelity posterior gets sharper in regions you only explored cheaply.

But the reduction is not unlimited, and the formula says why. Even with infinitely dense low-fidelity data, fLf_LfL​ becomes known exactly and the remaining uncertainty about fHf_HfH​ is the uncertainty about δ\deltaδ:

vH⋆ ⟶ Var⁡[δ(x⋆)∣yH]as NL→∞.v_H^\star \ \longrightarrow\ \operatorname{Var}\big[\delta(x^\star)\mid y_H\big] \qquad\text{as } N_L\to\infty .vH⋆​ ⟶ Var[δ(x⋆)∣yH​]as NL​→∞.

That is the entire economics of multi-fidelity modeling. Cheap data buys you fLf_LfL​; it cannot buy you δ\deltaδ. Expensive data is what learns the discrepancy, and the number of expensive runs you need is set by how complicated δ\deltaδ is — its amplitude and its length scale — not by how complicated fHf_HfH​ is. When the coarse model captures the physics and errs smoothly, δ\deltaδ is simple and a few high-fidelity runs suffice. When the coarse model is wrong in an intricate way, δ\deltaδ is as hard as fHf_HfH​ and you have gained nothing.

7. When the linear relation fails

The model assumes the two fidelities are related by one scalar ρ\rhoρ, uniformly across the input space. Real pairs of models often are not: a coarse mesh may track the fine one well at low Reynolds number and poorly at high, or the relation between them may simply be curved.

The generalization replaces the linear map by a learned nonlinear one:

ft(x)=gt(x, ft−1(x)),gt∼GP(0,kt),f_t(x) = g_t\big(x,\ f_{t-1}(x)\big), \qquad g_t\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k_t),ft​(x)=gt​(x, ft−1​(x)),gt​∼GP(0,kt​),

where gtg_tgt​ takes both the input and the lower-fidelity value as arguments. The standard kernel for gtg_tgt​ is a composition built with the rules of Lecture 9 §4.1,

kt((x,f),(x′,f′))=kt,x(x,x′)⋅kt,f(f,f′)  +  kt,δ(x,x′),k_t\big((x,f),(x',f')\big) = k_{t,x}(x,x')\cdot k_{t,f}(f,f') \;+\; k_{t,\delta}(x,x'),kt​((x,f),(x′,f′))=kt,x​(x,x′)⋅kt,f​(f,f′)+kt,δ​(x,x′),

a product term expressing "close in input and in low-fidelity value" plus an additive discrepancy. Setting kt,fk_{t,f}kt,f​ to a linear kernel recovers the autoregressive model, so this is a strict generalization.

The price. Composing a GP with a GP is no longer a GP: the low-fidelity input to gtg_tgt​ is itself uncertain, so the predictive distribution at a test point is not Gaussian and must be obtained by sampling — draw ft−1(x⋆)f_{t-1}(x^\star)ft−1​(x⋆) from its posterior, push each draw through gtg_tgt​, and accumulate. Exact conditioning is lost; everything else about the workflow survives.

8. Design, diagnostics, and where this goes

Check the assumption before trusting the model. If you have evaluated both fidelities at some common inputs, plot yHy_HyH​ against yLy_LyL​ at those points. A roughly linear scatter supports the autoregressive model; a visibly curved or fanning scatter says the relation is nonlinear and §7 is needed. This takes one line of code and settles a modeling question that would otherwise be argued about.

Read the fitted hyperparameters. They are interpretable, as always:

Where this goes. Bayesian optimization and active learning choose the next evaluation using μ⋆\mu^\starμ⋆ and v⋆v^\starv⋆. In the multi-fidelity setting the choice is richer: not only where to evaluate but at which fidelity, trading a small reduction in uncertainty now for a cheap price against a large reduction for an expensive one.

Quiz-eligible facts

  1. A multi-output GP is an ordinary GP on the augmented input space X×{1,…,T}\mathcal{X}\times\{1,\dots,T\}X×{1,…,T}, with kernel k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Cov⁡[ft(x),ft′(x′)]k((x,t),(x',t')) = \operatorname{Cov}[f_t(x),f_{t'}(x')]k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Cov[ft​(x),ft′​(x′)]; training, inference, and computation are unchanged from the single-output case.
  2. The stacked covariance has T×TT\times TT×T blocks; the off-diagonal blocks are what let data on one output inform another. Zero off-diagonals means TTT independent GPs.
  3. Outputs need not be observed at the same inputs; the blocks are simply rectangular.
  4. ICM: k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Btt′k(x,x′)k((x,t),(x',t')) = B_{tt'}k(x,x')k((x,t),(x′,t′))=Btt′​k(x,x′) with B⪰0B\succeq0B⪰0. Writing B=WW⊤B = WW^\topB=WW⊤ shows every output is a linear combination of shared latent GPs, which is why BBB must be positive semi-definite.
  5. ICM forces all outputs to share a length scale and smoothness, with correlation constant across input space. The LMC relaxes this by summing several separable terms with different kernels.
  6. The autoregressive multi-fidelity model is fH=ρfL+δf_H = \rho f_L+\deltafH​=ρfL​+δ with fL⊥δf_L\perp\deltafL​⊥δ, giving Cov⁡[fL,fL′]=kL\operatorname{Cov}[f_L,f_L'] = k_LCov[fL​,fL′​]=kL​, Cov⁡[fH,fL′]=ρkL\operatorname{Cov}[f_H,f_L'] = \rho k_LCov[fH​,fL′​]=ρkL​, and Cov⁡[fH,fH′]=ρ2kL+kδ\operatorname{Cov}[f_H,f_H'] = \rho^2k_L+k_\deltaCov[fH​,fH′​]=ρ2kL​+kδ​.
  7. It is an LMC with two latent processes, weights a=(1,ρ)a = (1,\rho)a=(1,ρ) and b=(0,1)b = (0,1)b=(0,1), so B(1)=aa⊤B^{(1)} = aa^\topB(1)=aa⊤ is rank one and B(2)=bb⊤B^{(2)} = bb^\topB(2)=bb⊤ assigns the discrepancy to the high fidelity alone.

  9. Joint training costs O((NL+NH)3)O((N_L+N_H)^3)O((NL​+NH​)3). The recursive scheme — fit fLf_LfL​, then fit a GP to the residuals yH−ρμL(XH)y_H-\rho\mu_L(X_H)yH​−ρμL​(XH​) — costs O(NL3)+O(NH3)O(N_L^3)+O(N_H^3)O(NL3​)+O(NH3​) and is exact for nested designs XH⊆XLX_H\subseteq X_LXH​⊆XL​.

P2. You fit the model and obtain ρ^=0.02\hat\rho = 0.02ρ^​=0.02, with a discrepancy amplitude about equal to the standard deviation of yHy_HyH​. What has the model concluded, and what should you check next?

Answer. That the two fidelities are essentially unrelated — with ρ≈0\rho\approx0ρ≈0 the off-diagonal blocks vanish and the fit has decoupled into two independent GPs, so the cheap data is contributing nothing. Before abandoning the cheap model, plot yHy_HyH​ against yLy_LyL​ at shared inputs: a curved relation would mean the fidelities are informative but not linearly so, calling for the nonlinear model rather than for more high-fidelity runs. (§§7–8)

P3. With NHN_HNH​ fixed, you increase NLN_LNL​ from 50 to 50,000. Does the high-fidelity predictive variance go to zero? What does it approach?

Answer. No. In the limit fLf_LfL​ becomes known exactly, so the remaining uncertainty is uncertainty about the discrepancy: vH⋆→Var⁡[δ(x⋆)∣yH]v_H^\star\to\operatorname{Var}[\delta(x^\star)\mid y_H]vH⋆​→Var[δ(x⋆)∣yH​]. Cheap data buys fLf_LfL​ only; δ\deltaδ can be learned only from high-fidelity data. (§6)

P4. Two coarse models are available for the same expensive simulation. Model A is biased but its error is smooth and slowly varying; model B is unbiased on average but its error oscillates rapidly. With a fixed budget of five high-fidelity runs, which makes the better low-fidelity level?

Answer. Model A. What the high-fidelity data must learn is δ\deltaδ, so the relevant question is how complicated the error is, not how accurate the model is. A smooth, slowly varying discrepancy is pinned down by a few points; a rapidly oscillating one needs about as many points as learning fHf_HfH​ from scratch, however small it is on average. (§6)

P5. Why does a nested design XH⊆XLX_H\subseteq X_LXH​⊆XL​ make recursive training exact, and what else does it buy you?

Answer. The recursive scheme fits the discrepancy to yH−ρμL(XH)y_H-\rho\mu_L(X_H)yH​−ρμL​(XH​), which requires the low-fidelity model's prediction at every high-fidelity input; when those inputs are a subset of XLX_LXL​ the low-fidelity posterior there is pinned down by actual data rather than interpolated, and the two-stage fit reproduces the joint model. It also gives you the shared evaluations needed for the yHy_HyH​-versus-yLy_LyL​ diagnostic of §8. (§§5.2, 8)
