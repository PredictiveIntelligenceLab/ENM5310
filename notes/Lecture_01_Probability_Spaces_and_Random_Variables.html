1. Why probability is the language of this course

Every dataset you will meet this semester is a finite collection of numbers produced by a process you do not fully control and do not fully understand. A wind-tunnel campaign, a batch of cell-viability assays, a molecular dynamics trajectory, a set of sensor readings from a turbine — in each case you observe

D={x1,…,xN},orD={(xn,yn)}n=1N,\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \mathcal{D} = \{(x_n, y_n)\}_{n=1}^N,D={x1​,…,xN​},orD={(xn​,yn​)}n=1N​,

and you want to say something about the next measurement, or about the mechanism, or about what to do next. Nothing in the finite list of numbers tells you how to do this. What licenses the leap from a finite sample to a statement about unseen cases is a modeling assumption: that the data are realizations of a repeatable experiment with random outcomes, governed by a distribution p⋆p^\starp⋆ that we do not know.

That single assumption is what the rest of this course is built on. It shows up in every method we will study, and the four questions we will ask of every method start from it:

  1. What is the probabilistic model? — what family of distributions are we willing to entertain?
  2. What is the learning principle? — what functional of the data do we optimize to select one?
  3. What is the approximation? — what do we do when that optimization is intractable?
  4. What is the computation? — what does it cost, and does it converge?

Today we build the vocabulary for question 1. Everything else follows.

Two readings of "probability." When we say a coin lands heads with probability 1/21/21/2, we can mean two different things.

  • The frequentist reading: in a long run of repeated flips, the fraction of heads tends to 1/21/21/2. Probability is a property of a repeatable physical setup.
  • The Bayesian reading: probability quantifies our state of information about a proposition. It applies to one-off events — "the fatigue crack propagates past 2 mm before 10⁶ cycles," "this catalyst screening campaign will find a candidate" — which have no long-run frequency at all.

We will use both, and it will matter which one is in play. But the calculus is identical under both readings; the axioms in §2 do not care. Do not let interpretive debates delay you.

Two kinds of uncertainty. A distinction we will return to in nearly every lecture:

  • Aleatoric (data) uncertainty: intrinsic variability that more data cannot remove. Even knowing the coin is exactly fair, you cannot predict the next flip. Measurement noise, turbulence, thermal fluctuation.
  • Epistemic (model) uncertainty: ignorance about parameters or mechanism, which does shrink as data accumulate.

Keep this split in mind. A model that reports a single number is silent about both. A model that reports one uncertainty band without saying which kind it is has told you something, but not enough to act on. In Lecture 3 we will see that the tool for cleanly separating them is mutual information.

2. Experiments, outcomes, events: the probability space

Formalize "an experiment with random outcomes" as a triple (Ω,F,P)(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})(Ω,F,P).

The sample space Ω\OmegaΩ is the set of all possible outcomes of one run of the experiment. Each ω∈Ω\omega \in \Omegaω∈Ω is a complete description of one run: a single coin flip, a single trajectory, a single manufactured part with all its properties. Ω\OmegaΩ can be finite ({H,T}\{H, T\}{H,T}), countable (N\mathbb{N}N, e.g. the number of defects), or uncountable (Rd\mathbb{R}^dRd, C([0,T])C([0,T])C([0,T]) for a random field).

The event space F\mathcal{F}F is the collection of subsets of Ω\OmegaΩ to which we are willing to assign probabilities. An event is a set of outcomes: "the part fails inspection," "the temperature exceeded 400 K." F\mathcal{F}F is required to be a σ\sigmaσ-algebra: it contains Ω\OmegaΩ, is closed under complement, and is closed under countable unions. Those three closure properties are exactly what you need so that "not AAA," "AAA or BBB," and "AAA and BBB" are again events with well-defined probabilities.

Why not just take F=2Ω\mathcal{F} = 2^\OmegaF=2Ω? For finite or countable Ω\OmegaΩ you can, and we will. For Ω=R\Omega = \mathbb{R}Ω=R you cannot: there is no way to assign a length to every subset of R\mathbb{R}R that is simultaneously translation-invariant and countably additive (Vitali sets). The resolution is to restrict to the Borel σ\sigmaσ-algebra — generated by intervals — which contains every set you will ever write down. This is the entire practical content of measure theory for our purposes. We will not prove anything about it and we will not mention it again except when it bites (and it will bite exactly once, in Lecture 3, when we discuss differential entropy).

The probability measure P:F→[0,1]\mathbb{P}: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]P:F→[0,1] satisfies the Kolmogorov axioms:

(i)P(A)≥0,(ii)P(Ω)=1,(iii)P(⋃i=1∞Ai)=∑i=1∞P(Ai)  for disjoint Ai.\text{(i)}\quad \mathbb{P}(A) \ge 0, \qquad \text{(ii)}\quad \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1, \qquad \text{(iii)}\quad \mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\Big) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}(A_i) \ \text{ for disjoint } A_i.(i)P(A)≥0,(ii)P(Ω)=1,(iii)P(i=1⋃∞​Ai​)=i=1∑∞​P(Ai​)  for disjoint Ai​.

Everything else in elementary probability is a consequence. Two you should be able to reproduce cold:

P(Aˉ)=1−P(A),P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B).\mathbb{P}(\bar A) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(A), \qquad \mathbb{P}(A \cup B) = \mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B) - \mathbb{P}(A \cap B).P(Aˉ)=1−P(A),P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B).

Conditioning. For P(A)>0\mathbb{P}(A) > 0P(A)>0,

P(B∣A)≜P(A∩B)P(A).\mathbb{P}(B \mid A) \triangleq \frac{\mathbb{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A)}.P(B∣A)≜P(A)P(A∩B)​.

Read this geometrically: conditioning restricts the sample space to AAA and renormalizes so the restricted space has total mass 1. It is not a new axiom; it is a definition, and it is the single most important definition in the course. Undefined when P(A)=0\mathbb{P}(A) = 0P(A)=0 — which is why conditioning on continuous observations needs a little more care than the discrete formula suggests, a subtlety we will handle with densities rather than with measure theory.

Independence. Conditional independence is the workhorse — unconditional independence is rare in real systems, while conditional independence given a well-chosen intermediate quantity is the structural assumption behind essentially every scalable model we will build.

3. Random variables and their laws

In practice we never work with Ω\OmegaΩ. We work with measurements of outcomes.

Definition. A random variable is a (measurable) function X:Ω→XX : \Omega \to \mathcal{X}X:Ω→X. Its law or distribution is the pushforward measure

PX(B)≜P(X−1(B))=P({ω:X(ω)∈B}),B⊆X. \mathbb{P}_X(B) \triangleq \mathbb{P}\big(X^{-1}(B)\big) = \mathbb{P}(\{\omega : X(\omega) \in B\}), \qquad B \subseteq \mathcal{X}. PX​(B)≜P(X−1(B))=P({ω:X(ω)∈B}),B⊆X.

Three objects, routinely conflated, that you must keep separate:

Object Symbol Type
the random variable XXX a function on Ω\OmegaΩ
its law PX\mathbb{P}_XPX​, or its density p(x)p(x)p(x) a measure / function on X\mathcal{X}X
a realization x=X(ω)x = X(\omega)x=X(ω) a number you can print

A dataset is a list of realizations. A model is a claim about the law. Fitting is choosing a law given realizations. If you keep this triple straight, half the notational confusion in machine learning papers evaporates.

The point of the definition: once we have PX\mathbb{P}_XPX​, the underlying Ω\OmegaΩ becomes irrelevant. Every probability statement about XXX is a statement about its law. From here on we write p(x)p(x)p(x) and never mention ω\omegaω again. Measure theory is the scaffolding; we take it down once the building stands.

A note on notation, since ML papers are sloppy about it and you should not be: p(x)p(x)p(x) and p(y)p(y)p(y) can denote two different functions, disambiguated only by their argument name. When ambiguity threatens, write pX(⋅)p_X(\cdot)pX​(⋅) and pY(⋅)p_Y(\cdot)pY​(⋅). In derivations involving change of variables (Lecture 3), the sloppy convention will cost you.

5. Expectation, moments, and what they hide

Definition. The expectation of XXX is

E[X]≜∫Xx p(x) dx(continuous),E[X]≜∑x∈Xx p(x)(discrete),\mathbb{E}[X] \triangleq \int_{\mathcal{X}} x\, p(x)\,dx \quad \text{(continuous)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] \triangleq \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} x\, p(x) \quad \text{(discrete)},E[X]≜∫X​xp(x)dx(continuous),E[X]≜x∈X∑​xp(x)(discrete),

provided the integral converges absolutely. If it does not, the mean does not exist — this is not a pathology confined to textbooks; the Cauchy distribution (Lecture 3) has no mean, and heavy-tailed empirical distributions arise in materials failure, network traffic, and loss landscapes.

5.1 What moments hide

Summary statistics are lossy, and the loss is not benign. Anscombe's quartet (Murphy Fig. 2.5) is four datasets of eleven points with identical E[x],V[x],E[y],V[y]\mathbb{E}[x], \mathbb{V}[x], \mathbb{E}[y], \mathbb{V}[y]E[x],V[x],E[y],V[y] and identical correlation ρ=0.816\rho = 0.816ρ=0.816; one is linear, one is quadratic, one is linear with a single outlier, one is a vertical stack plus a lever point. The Datasaurus Dozen (Fig. 2.6) pushes this to its logical extreme: twelve datasets with matching low-order statistics to two decimals, one of which is a dinosaur.

The pedagogical point is not "make plots." It is that any finite set of moments defines a level set of datasets, and that level set is enormous. When you report a mean squared error, you are reporting one number from a level set that contains both a well-calibrated model and a model that is catastrophically wrong on a subpopulation. This is the first appearance of a theme that runs through the whole course: aggregate metrics are hypotheses about model behavior, not evidence of it.

6. The empirical distribution: where data re-enters

We began by assuming the data are realizations from an unknown p⋆p^\starp⋆. Here is the object that connects the two.

Definition. Given D={x(1),…,x(N)}\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}\}D={x(1),…,x(N)}, the empirical distribution is

p^N(x)≜1N∑n=1Nδx(n)(x),\hat p_N(x) \triangleq \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \delta_{x^{(n)}}(x),p^​N​(x)≜N1​n=1∑N​δx(n)​(x),

where δy\delta_yδy​ is the Dirac delta at yyy (formally: the measure placing mass 1/N1/N1/N at each observed point). Its cdf is the empirical cdf

P^N(x)=1N∑n=1NI(x(n)≤x),\hat P_N(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N \mathbb{I}\big(x^{(n)} \le x\big),P^N​(x)=N1​n=1∑N​I(x(n)≤x),

a staircase with jumps of height 1/N1/N1/N at each sample.

The empirical distribution is a bona fide probability distribution — over the data you happened to collect. Its crucial property:

Ep^N[f(X)]=1N∑n=1Nf(x(n)).\mathbb{E}_{\hat p_N}[f(X)] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N f\big(x^{(n)}\big).Ep^​N​​[f(X)]=N1​n=1∑N​f(x(n)).

Expectation under p^N\hat p_Np^​N​ is exactly the sample average. Every empirical risk you will minimize this semester — mean squared error, cross-entropy, the ELBO's reconstruction term — is an expectation under p^N\hat p_Np^​N​ standing in for the expectation under p⋆p^\starp⋆ that you actually want. This substitution is called the plug-in principle, and naming it now buys you a precise vocabulary for what goes wrong later:

  • Generalization gap = the difference between Ep⋆\mathbb{E}_{p^\star}Ep⋆​ and Ep^N\mathbb{E}_{\hat p_N}Ep^​N​​ of the same loss.
  • Overfitting = choosing a model flexible enough to exploit the difference between p^N\hat p_Np^​N​ and p⋆p^\starp⋆.
  • Distribution shift = deploying under a p⋆p^\starp⋆ that is not the one p^N\hat p_Np^​N​ was drawn from.

Three ways of saying the same thing, and all three are statements about the gap between a staircase and a smooth curve.

7. Monte Carlo and the N−1/2N^{-1/2}N−1/2 rate

Why this is the central fact of computational statistics. Compare against deterministic quadrature. A tensor-product rule of order rrr in ddd dimensions with NNN total nodes has error O(N−r/d)O(N^{-r/d})O(N−r/d) — it degrades exponentially in dimension. The Monte Carlo rate O(N−1/2)O(N^{-1/2})O(N−1/2) contains no ddd at all. It is slow (a decimal digit costs 100×100\times100× the work) but it is dimension-free.

This asymmetry is why the entire probabilistic side of machine learning is built on sampling: for a neural network with 10810^8108 parameters, the posterior expectation ∫f(θ)p(θ∣D) dθ\int f(\theta) p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\,d\theta∫f(θ)p(θ∣D)dθ is a 10810^8108-dimensional integral. No quadrature scheme will ever touch it. Sampling will — badly, slowly, but it will. Everything we do in the variational inference and MCMC portions of this course is an attempt to make σf\sigma_fσf​ smaller or to sample from ppp when we cannot sample from ppp directly.

Caveat to flag now, cash out later. The bound assumes (i) σf2<∞\sigma_f^2 < \inftyσf2​<∞ and (ii) genuinely independent draws. Heavy-tailed fff breaks (i); MCMC breaks (ii) and replaces NNN with an effective sample size that can be orders of magnitude smaller. When someone shows you an MC error bar, ask which of these they checked.
