Several Random Variables: Dependence, Conditioning, and Bayes' Rule

Reading: Murphy, PML: An Introduction, §2.2.3–2.2.5, §2.3; §3.1–3.2 for the multivariate Gaussian. Builds on: Lecture 1 (probability spaces, random variables, laws, expectation, empirical distribution).

Learning objectives. You should be able to (i) factor a joint distribution and state what each factorization assumes; (ii) explain why conditional independence is what makes high-dimensional modeling possible; (iii) compute covariances and say precisely what correlation does and does not detect; (iv) prove that the conditional mean minimizes squared error and identify the irreducible term; (v) apply Bayes' rule to a diagnostic problem and explain base-rate neglect quantitatively; (vi) condition a joint Gaussian and recognize the formula as the engine behind GP regression and the Kalman filter.

1. Joints, marginals, conditionals — and Bayes

Everything interesting involves more than one quantity: input and output, latent state and observation, parameter and data. For two random variables X,YX, YX,Y, the joint distribution p(x,y)p(x,y)p(x,y) specifies the whole story. In the finite case it is a table with non-negative entries summing to one.

p(X,Y)Y=0Y=1X=00.20.3X=10.30.2 \begin{array}{c|cc} p(X,Y) & Y=0 & Y=1\\\hline X=0 & 0.2 & 0.3\\ X=1 & 0.3 & 0.2 \end{array} p(X,Y)X=0X=1​Y=00.20.3​Y=10.30.2​​

From the joint, three operations generate everything else.

Marginalization (sum rule). To discard a variable, integrate it out:

p(x)=∑yp(x,y),p(x)=∫p(x,y) dy.p(x) = \sum_y p(x,y), \qquad p(x) = \int p(x,y)\,dy.p(x)=y∑​p(x,y),p(x)=∫p(x,y)dy.

The name comes from accountants writing row and column sums in the margin of the table. Trivial to write, often computationally brutal — much of the second half of this course exists because marginalizing over latent variables or parameters is intractable.

Conditioning (product rule).

p(y∣x)=p(x,y)p(x)⟺p(x,y)=p(x) p(y∣x).p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y\mid x).p(y∣x)=p(x)p(x,y)​⟺p(x,y)=p(x)p(y∣x).

Geometrically: pick out one row of the table and renormalize it so it sums to one. Conditioning is restrict and rescale, nothing more.

Chain rule. Iterating the product rule over DDD variables:

p(x1:D)=p(x1) p(x2∣x1) p(x3∣x1,x2)⋯p(xD∣x1:D−1).p(x_{1:D}) = p(x_1)\,p(x_2\mid x_1)\,p(x_3\mid x_1,x_2)\cdots p(x_D \mid x_{1:D-1}).p(x1:D​)=p(x1​)p(x2​∣x1​)p(x3​∣x1​,x2​)⋯p(xD​∣x1:D−1​).

This is an identity — no assumptions. It is also the architectural principle behind every autoregressive model: a language model, a PixelCNN, the reverse chain of a diffusion model. Each is the chain rule with the conditionals parameterized by a network. Nothing has been assumed yet; modeling begins when we choose to truncate or restrict these conditionals.

Bayes' rule. The product rule factors the same joint two ways, p(x,y)=p(x) p(y∣x)=p(y) p(x∣y)p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y|x) = p(y)\,p(x|y)p(x,y)=p(x)p(y∣x)=p(y)p(x∣y). Equate and divide:

 p(x∣y)=p(y∣x) p(x)p(y),p(y)=∑x′p(y∣x′) p(x′) \boxed{\ p(x \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)\,p(x)}{p(y)}, \qquad p(y) = \sum_{x'} p(y\mid x')\,p(x')\ } p(x∣y)=p(y)p(y∣x)p(x)​,p(y)=x′∑​p(y∣x′)p(x′) ​

That is the entire derivation — one line, with no content beyond the product rule. What Bayes' rule buys is a direction reversal: it converts knowledge of p(y∣x)p(y\mid x)p(y∣x), which is what a physical model or a measurement device gives you, into p(x∣y)p(x\mid y)p(x∣y), which is what you want. We use it as an identity for today's mechanics and return in §6 to what it means when xxx is an unknown parameter rather than another observable.

2. Independence, conditional independence, and parameter counting

X⊥Y  ⟺  p(x,y)=p(x)p(y);X⊥Y∣Z  ⟺  p(x,y∣z)=p(x∣z) p(y∣z).X \perp Y \iff p(x,y) = p(x)p(y); \qquad X \perp Y \mid Z \iff p(x,y\mid z) = p(x\mid z)\,p(y\mid z).X⊥Y⟺p(x,y)=p(x)p(y);X⊥Y∣Z⟺p(x,y∣z)=p(x∣z)p(y∣z).

For a set X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​, mutual independence requires factorization over every subset, not just pairs. Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence (Practice Set 0.2, Q1; three binary variables suffice).

Why factorization is the whole game

Take XXX with 6 states and YYY with 5. A general joint needs 6×5−1=296\times5-1 = 296×5−1=29 free parameters. Assume independence and you need (6−1)+(5−1)=9(6-1)+(5-1) = 9(6−1)+(5−1)=9. Now scale: DDD binary variables give a joint table with 2D−12^D-12D−1 free parameters. At D=100D=100D=100 that exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe, and you have N∼104N \sim 10^4N∼104 data points.

High-dimensional joint distributions are learnable only because they factorize. Every model in this course is a choice of factorization — a set of conditional independence assumptions — plus a parameterization of the surviving conditionals.

Unconditional independence is almost always false in engineering systems: everything influences everything. Conditional independence is often nearly true, because influence is mediated: X→Z→YX \to Z \to YX→Z→Y. Given the intermediate state ZZZ, the endpoints decouple. That graph is the simplest graphical model.

Instances you already know: the Markov property in a dynamical system (xt+1⊥xt−1∣xtx_{t+1}\perp x_{t-1}\mid x_txt+1​⊥xt−1​∣xt​); naive Bayes (features conditionally independent given the label); the mean-field family in variational inference (latents independent given data). Each is a factorization chosen for tractability, and each is wrong in a way you must be able to diagnose.

3. Covariance and correlation: measuring linear dependence

Independence is a yes/no property of the entire joint. We also want a number summarizing how two variables move together.

Definition. Cov[X,Y]≜E[(X−μX)(Y−μY)]=E[XY]−μXμY.\displaystyle \mathrm{Cov}[X,Y] \triangleq \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y.Cov[X,Y]≜E[(X−μX​)(Y−μY​)]=E[XY]−μX​μY​.

Read the definition rather than memorizing it. The product (X−μX)(Y−μY)(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)(X−μX​)(Y−μY​) is positive whenever both variables sit on the same side of their means, negative when they sit on opposite sides. Covariance is the average of that product: positive if the pair tends to deviate together, negative if oppositely, zero if the two tendencies cancel. On the board: draw the scatter cloud, put crosshairs at (μX,μY)(\mu_X,\mu_Y)(μX​,μY​), label the quadrants +,−,−,++,-,-,++,−,−,+. Covariance is a mass-weighted vote among quadrants.

Properties to have at hand:

Cov[X,X]=V[X],Cov[aX+b, cY+d]=ac Cov[X,Y],\mathrm{Cov}[X,X] = \mathbb{V}[X], \qquad \mathrm{Cov}[aX+b,\ cY+d] = ac\,\mathrm{Cov}[X,Y],Cov[X,X]=V[X],Cov[aX+b, cY+d]=acCov[X,Y],
V[X+Y]=V[X]+V[Y]+2 Cov[X,Y].\mathbb{V}[X+Y] = \mathbb{V}[X] + \mathbb{V}[Y] + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}[X,Y].V[X+Y]=V[X]+V[Y]+2Cov[X,Y].

The last line explains why "variances add" required independence in Lecture 1: the cross term vanishes only when the covariance does.

Covariance matrix. For a random vector x∈Rdx\in\mathbb{R}^dx∈Rd with mean μ\muμ,

Σ≜Cov[x]=E[(x−μ)(x−μ)⊤],Σij=Cov[xi,xj].\Sigma \triangleq \mathrm{Cov}[x] = \mathbb{E}\big[(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^\top\big], \qquad \Sigma_{ij} = \mathrm{Cov}[x_i,x_j].Σ≜Cov[x]=E[(x−μ)(x−μ)⊤],Σij​=Cov[xi​,xj​].

Σ\SigmaΣ is symmetric and positive semi-definite, and the proof is one line that also hands you the most useful identity in the section: for any fixed aaa,

V[a⊤x]=a⊤Σa  ≥  0.\mathbb{V}[a^\top x] = a^\top \Sigma a \;\ge\; 0.V[a⊤x]=a⊤Σa≥0.

Every linear combination has non-negative variance, hence Σ⪰0\Sigma\succeq0Σ⪰0. More generally, for an affine map,

E[Ax+b]=Aμ+b,Cov[Ax+b]=AΣA⊤.\mathbb{E}[Ax+b] = A\mu+b, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}[Ax+b] = A\Sigma A^\top.E[Ax+b]=Aμ+b,Cov[Ax+b]=AΣA⊤.

Second moments transform by sandwiching. This one formula sits behind error propagation in measurement chains, the covariance update in a Kalman filter, and the whitening transform x~=Λ−1/2U⊤(x−μ)\tilde x = \Lambda^{-1/2}U^\top(x-\mu)x~=Λ−1/2U⊤(x−μ) built from the eigendecomposition Σ=UΛU⊤\Sigma = U\Lambda U^\topΣ=UΛU⊤, which produces uncorrelated unit-variance coordinates. PCA is that eigendecomposition read as a modeling tool.

Correlation. Covariance carries units of [X][Y][X][Y][X][Y], so its magnitude means nothing on its own. Normalize:

ρXY≜Cov[X,Y]σXσY∈[−1,1],\rho_{XY} \triangleq \frac{\mathrm{Cov}[X,Y]}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y} \in [-1,1],ρXY​≜σX​σY​Cov[X,Y]​∈[−1,1],

with the bound from Cauchy–Schwarz. The extremes are the informative part: ∣ρ∣=1|\rho| = 1∣ρ∣=1 iff Y=aX+bY = aX+bY=aX+b exactly (with probability one). So correlation measures proximity to an exact linear relationship, and that is all it measures.

The two things people get wrong here

(i) Independent ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ uncorrelated, but uncorrelated ⇏\not\Rightarrow⇒ independent. Let XXX be symmetric about zero and Y=X2Y = X^2Y=X2. Then

Cov[X,Y]=E[X3]−E[X] E[X2]=0,\mathrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0,Cov[X,Y]=E[X3]−E[X]E[X2]=0,

yet YYY is a deterministic function of XXX. Zero correlation, total dependence. Correlation is blind to any dependence that is not linear — symmetric, periodic, threshold, or heteroscedastic structure all pass undetected. This is the quantitative form of Lecture 1's Anscombe point: all four Anscombe datasets have ρ=0.816\rho=0.816ρ=0.816, including the one whose true relationship is a parabola and the one that is a vertical stack plus a single lever point.

The important exception: if (X,Y)(X,Y)(X,Y) are jointly Gaussian, uncorrelated does imply independent. For Gaussians the covariance is the entire dependence structure. That is a property of one family, not a general fact, and assuming it elsewhere is a common and expensive error.

(ii) Correlation is not causation, and it is also not prediction. ρ=0.3\rho = 0.3ρ=0.3 sounds weak but can carry real predictive value; ρ=0.95\rho=0.95ρ=0.95 computed on a dataset with one leverage point can vanish when that point is removed. Report the scatter plot next to the number, always.

From data. The plug-in principle (Lecture 1) applied to p^N\hat p_Np^​N​ gives the empirical covariance

Σ^=1N−1∑n=1N(xn−xˉ)(xn−xˉ)⊤,\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^N (x_n-\bar x)(x_n-\bar x)^\top,Σ^=N−11​n=1∑N​(xn​−xˉ)(xn​−xˉ)⊤,

with N−1N-1N−1 for the unbiased version and NNN for the plug-in/MLE version. Know which one your library uses. In ddd dimensions with N≲dN \lesssim dN≲d this matrix is singular, and every method that inverts it — Gaussian discriminant analysis, Mahalanobis distances, GP regression with a fitted covariance — fails in a way that regularization exists to fix.

4. Conditional expectation: your best guess given partial information

Once we can condition, we can take expectations under conditionals:

E[X∣Y=y]=∫x p(x∣y) dx.\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y] = \int x\,p(x\mid y)\,dx.E[X∣Y=y]=∫xp(x∣y)dx.

The intuition to hold onto: E[X∣Y=y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]E[X∣Y=y] is your best single guess about XXX if someone tells you Y=yY=yY=y and nothing else. Before the message arrives you do not know which yyy you will hear, so the guess-you-will-make is itself uncertain. That is why E[X∣Y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]E[X∣Y], as a function of the random YYY, is a random variable, whereas E[X∣Y=y]\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]E[X∣Y=y] is a number. Nearly all confusion in this material traces back to blurring those two.

Law of iterated expectations (tower property).

E[X]=EY[ E[X∣Y] ].\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}_Y\big[\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\,\big].E[X]=EY​[E[X∣Y]].

In words: if you average, over all the messages you might receive, the guess you would make after receiving each one, you recover the guess you would make with no message at all. Information changes your estimate, but it cannot change it on average, in advance — otherwise you should have moved your estimate already. (This is the seed of the martingale property, and it is why unbiased estimators stay unbiased under stratification.)

Proof (discrete, one line for the board):

EY[E[X∣Y]]=∑y(∑xx p(x∣y))p(y)=∑x,yx p(x,y)=E[X].■\mathbb{E}_Y\big[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]\big] = \sum_y\Big(\sum_x x\,p(x\mid y)\Big)p(y) = \sum_{x,y} x\,p(x,y) = \mathbb{E}[X].\qquad\blacksquareEY​[E[X∣Y]]=y∑​(x∑​xp(x∣y))p(y)=x,y∑​xp(x,y)=E[X].■

Concrete version. Bulbs from factory 1 last 5000 h on average, factory 2, 4000 h; factory 1 supplies 60%. Then E[lifetime]=0.6(5000)+0.4(4000)=4600\mathbb{E}[\text{lifetime}] = 0.6(5000)+0.4(4000) = 4600E[lifetime]=0.6(5000)+0.4(4000)=4600: average the group averages, weighted by group size. Every stratified estimator, importance-weighted correction, and minibatch gradient estimate is this identity.

Here's a drop-in replacement for the law-of-total-variance block in §4. The main change is proving it by splitting the deviation into two orthogonal pieces, which makes the result look inevitable rather than like an algebraic accident — and which sets up §5 so that the regression theorem costs almost nothing.

Law of total variance.

V[X]  =  EY[V[X∣Y]]⏟uncertainty that survives learning Y  +  VY[E[X∣Y]]⏟how much learning Y moves your guess\mathbb{V}[X] \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_Y\big[\mathbb{V}[X\mid Y]\big]}_{\text{uncertainty that survives learning } Y} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{V}_Y\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]}_{\text{how much learning } Y \text{ moves your guess}}V[X]=uncertainty that survives learning YEY​[V[X∣Y]]​​+how much learning Y moves your guessVY​[E[X∣Y]]​​

Where it comes from. Split the deviation of XXX from its mean into two pieces by inserting the conditional mean:

X−E[X]  =  (X−E[X∣Y])⏟how far X falls from the guess  +  (E[X∣Y]−E[X])⏟how far the guess movedX - \mathbb{E}[X] \;=\; \underbrace{\big(X - \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)}_{\text{how far } X \text{ falls from the guess}} \;+\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] - \mathbb{E}[X]\big)}_{\text{how far the guess moved}}X−E[X]=how far X falls from the guess(X−E[X∣Y])​​+how far the guess moved(E[X∣Y]−E[X])​​

These two pieces are uncorrelated. The second is a function of YYY alone, and the first has conditional mean zero given YYY, so conditioning on YYY and applying the tower property kills the cross term. Uncorrelated pieces have additive variances (§3), so take variances of both sides and the theorem falls out — it is the Pythagorean theorem, with the two legs being prediction error and guess movement.

Two ways to read it.

As two stages of randomness. Think of how a draw of XXX is actually generated: first nature picks a group YYY, then it draws XXX from within that group. Two stages, two contributions to the spread. If you only counted the within-group scatter you would be pretending every group sits at the same place; if you only counted the between-group scatter you would be pretending each group is a single point.

As the value of information. The tower property said learning YYY cannot change your estimate on average. This says something sharper: the amount your estimate moves around as YYY varies is exactly the amount of uncertainty YYY removes. An observation is informative precisely to the extent that different outcomes of it would send you to different conclusions. If every possible value of YYY would leave your best guess where it was, VY[E[X∣Y]]=0\mathbb{V}_Y[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]] = 0VY​[E[X∣Y]]=0 and the observation is worthless. This is the seed of expected information gain, and we will meet it again as an acquisition function.

Numbers. Yield strength of a bracket sourced equally from three suppliers, each with within-supplier standard deviation 10 MPa and means 300, 320, 340 MPa.

  • Within: EY[V[X∣Y]]=100\mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbb{V}[X|Y]] = 100EY​[V[X∣Y]]=100.
  • Between: grand mean 320, so VY[E[X∣Y]]=13[(−20)2+02+202]=266.7\mathbb{V}_Y[\mathbb{E}[X|Y]] = \tfrac13\big[(-20)^2 + 0^2 + 20^2\big] = 266.7VY​[E[X∣Y]]=31​[(−20)2+02+202]=266.7.
  • Total: 366.7366.7366.7, i.e. σ=19.1\sigma = 19.1σ=19.1 MPa across the whole supply, versus 101010 MPa once you know the supplier.

Knowing which supplier shipped the part removes 266.7/366.7=73%266.7/366.7 = 73\%266.7/366.7=73% of the variance. That fraction is R2R^2R2, and §7 will show it equals ρ2\rho^2ρ2 in the Gaussian case.

The two extremes are worth stating out loud. If the suppliers had identical means, the between term vanishes and the supplier label is uninformative. If each supplier were perfectly consistent, the within term vanishes and the label tells you everything. Real cases sit between, and the ratio is what "informative" means quantitatively.

The error this is designed to prevent. Averaging the conditional variances — reporting 101010 MPa as the spread of the parts you receive — understates the true spread by nearly a factor of two, because it forgets that the groups sit in different places. The same mistake in a predictive model is reporting the average within-model uncertainty of an ensemble while ignoring how much the ensemble members disagree with each other. That disagreement is the second term.

Why this decomposition earns its lecture time. Set Y=θY = \thetaY=θ, the parameters:

V[y⋆]=Eθ[V[y⋆∣θ]]⏟aleatoric+Vθ[E[y⋆∣θ]]⏟epistemic\mathbb{V}[y_\star] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\mathbb{V}[y_\star\mid\theta]\big]}_{\text{aleatoric}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{V}_\theta\big[\mathbb{E}[y_\star\mid\theta]\big]}_{\text{epistemic}}V[y⋆​]=aleatoricEθ​[V[y⋆​∣θ]]​​+epistemicVθ​[E[y⋆​∣θ]]​​

Noise that persists even if you knew the model, plus disagreement among the models the data cannot rule out. This is Lecture 1's split with an equation attached, and a deep ensemble estimates the second term by brute force. The bias–variance decomposition is the same algebra with YYY = the training set.

5. Regression is a conditional expectation

This is the theorem that says what supervised learning is actually computing.

Theorem. Among all measurable functions fff, the minimizer of E[(Y−f(X))2]\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-f(X))^2\big]E[(Y−f(X))2] is

f⋆(x)=E[Y∣X=x].f^\star(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x].f⋆(x)=E[Y∣X=x].

The intuitive argument. Use the tower property to take the expectation over XXX on the outside:

E[(Y−f(X))2]=EX[ E[(Y−f(x))2 ∣ X=x]⏟an inner problem at fixed x ].\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-f(X))^2\big] = \mathbb{E}_X\Big[\ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-f(x))^2\ \big|\ X=x\big]}_{\text{an inner problem at fixed } x}\ \Big].E[(Y−f(X))2]=EX​[ an inner problem at fixed xE[(Y−f(x))2 ∣∣​ X=x]​​ ].

Look at the inner expectation. For a fixed xxx, the value f(x)f(x)f(x) is just a constant ccc, so the question becomes: which constant best predicts the random variable Y∣X=xY\mid X=xY∣X=x under squared error? You can already answer that — the best constant predictor of any random variable is its mean, because

E[(Y−c)2∣x]=V[Y∣x]+(E[Y∣x]−c)2,\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-c)^2\mid x\big] = \mathbb{V}[Y\mid x] + \big(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid x]-c\big)^2,E[(Y−c)2∣x]=V[Y∣x]+(E[Y∣x]−c)2,

minimized at c=E[Y∣x]c=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid x]c=E[Y∣x] with residual V[Y∣x]\mathbb{V}[Y\mid x]V[Y∣x]. The outer average is a sum of non-negative terms, and fff is free to pick a different constant at each xxx, so the problem decouples: solve it separately in every vertical slice. Picture the scatter cloud; above each xxx sits a slice with distribution p(y∣x)p(y\mid x)p(y∣x); the optimal curve threads the mean of every slice. Averaging back over xxx:

E[(Y−f(X))2]=E[V[Y∣X]]⏟irreducible+E[(E[Y∣X]−f(X))2]⏟≥0, =0 iff f=f⋆.■\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-f(X))^2\big] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{V}[Y|X]\big]}_{\text{irreducible}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbb{E}[Y|X]-f(X))^2\big]}_{\ge0,\ =0\ \text{iff}\ f=f^\star}.\qquad\blacksquareE[(Y−f(X))2]=irreducibleE[V[Y∣X]]​​+≥0, =0 iff f=f⋆E[(E[Y∣X]−f(X))2]​​.■

The geometric version, in one sentence. The identity E[(Y−E[Y∣X]) g(X)]=0\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|X])\,g(X)\big]=0E[(Y−E[Y∣X])g(X)]=0 for every ggg says the residual is orthogonal to every function of XXX. So E[Y∣X]\mathbb{E}[Y|X]E[Y∣X] is the orthogonal projection of YYY onto the space of functions of XXX, and the decomposition above is the Pythagorean theorem in that space. If projections are intuitive to you, that is the fastest way to hold the whole result.

Three consequences you will meet in your own experiments:

  1. Least squares does not target YYY; it targets E[Y∣X]\mathbb{E}[Y|X]E[Y∣X]. A perfect regressor still has test MSE equal to E[V[Y∣X]]\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{V}[Y|X]]E[V[Y∣X]]. If you do not know that floor for your dataset, you cannot distinguish a converged model from an underfit one, and you will lose weeks to the difference.
  2. The floor is aleatoric. More data, bigger models, and longer training do not reduce it. Only better inputs — measuring more of what YYY actually depends on — do. When a training curve plateaus, the first question is whether it plateaued at the noise floor.
  3. The conditional mean is a poor summary of a multimodal conditional. If p(y∣x)p(y|x)p(y∣x) is bimodal, E[Y∣X=x]\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]E[Y∣X=x] lands in the valley between the modes and may be a physically impossible output. This is why L2L_2L2​-trained models produce blurry images and over-smoothed turbulent fields, and why we build generative models of p(y∣x)p(y|x)p(y∣x) instead of point predictors. The blur is not an architecture bug; it is this theorem working correctly.

Your loss chooses which summary you get. Minimizing E∣Y−f(X)∣\mathbb{E}|Y-f(X)|E∣Y−f(X)∣ returns the conditional median; the pinball loss at level τ\tauτ returns the conditional τ\tauτ-quantile; a full negative log-likelihood (Lecture 3) returns the whole conditional distribution. Choose deliberately.

6. Bayes as inference

§1 gave Bayes' rule as an identity relating two observables. It becomes inference when the conditioned variable is an unknown we want to learn — a parameter, a hypothesis, a hidden state:

p(h∣y)=p(h) p(y∣h)p(y),p(y)=∑h′p(h′) p(y∣h′).p(h\mid y) = \frac{p(h)\,p(y\mid h)}{p(y)}, \qquad p(y) = \sum_{h'} p(h')\,p(y\mid h').p(h∣y)=p(y)p(h)p(y∣h)​,p(y)=h′∑​p(h′)p(y∣h′).
Term Name Read as
p(h)p(h)p(h) prior belief before data

The most common error in this material: the likelihood p(y∣h)p(y\mid h)p(y∣h), read as a function of hhh, is not a distribution over hhh. It does not integrate to one over hhh, and p(y∣h)=0.9p(y|h)=0.9p(y∣h)=0.9 does not mean "hhh is 90% likely." Maximum likelihood selects the hhh under which the observed data are most probable — a different statement from selecting the most probable hhh. The two coincide only under a flat prior.

6.1 Diagnostic testing and base rates

Now set π=0.01\pi = 0.01π=0.01 and redo it: the posteriors become 26% and 0.13%. A positive result from a 97.5%-specific test leaves you more likely healthy than sick.

Natural frequencies make it obvious. Out of 100,000 people at π=0.01\pi=0.01π=0.01: 1,000 infected, of whom 0.875(1000)=8750.875(1000) = 8750.875(1000)=875 test positive; 99,000 healthy, of whom 0.025(99,000)=2,4750.025(99{,}000) = 2{,}4750.025(99,000)=2,475 test positive. The general statement: when the base rate is small, false positives drawn from the large negative population swamp true positives from the small positive one. Replace "infected" with defective weld, anomalous sensor reading, hit compound in a screening library, flagged transaction. Any rare-event detector must be judged against its base rate, not against sensitivity and specificity alone — which is also why accuracy is useless on imbalanced data and why we will use precision–recall and calibration curves instead.

6.2 Monty Hall

You pick door 1; the host — who knows the prize location and never reveals it — opens door 3. With p(Hi)=1/3p(H_i)=1/3p(Hi​)=1/3 and host policy p(Y=3∣H1)=12p(Y{=}3|H_1)=\tfrac12p(Y=3∣H1​)=21​, p(Y=3∣H2)=1p(Y{=}3|H_2)=1p(Y=3∣H2​)=1, p(Y=3∣H3)=0p(Y{=}3|H_3)=0p(Y=3∣H3​)=0, we get p(Y=3)=12p(Y{=}3)=\tfrac12p(Y=3)=21​, hence p(H1∣Y=3)=13p(H_1|Y{=}3)=\tfrac13p(H1​∣Y=3)=31​ and p(H2∣Y=3)=23p(H_2|Y{=}3)=\tfrac23p(H2​∣Y=3)=32​. Switch.

The lesson is not the puzzle. It is that the likelihood encodes the data-collection protocol. Had the host opened a door at random and happened to miss the prize, p(Y=3∣H1)=p(Y=3∣H2)=12p(Y{=}3|H_1)=p(Y{=}3|H_2)=\tfrac12p(Y=3∣H1​)=p(Y=3∣H2​)=21​ and the posterior would be 12\tfrac1221​–12\tfrac1221​: switching gains nothing. Identical observation, different answer. Carry this into your own work — nonrandom missingness, stopping rules, selection on the outcome, and benchmark curation all change p(y∣h)p(y|h)p(y∣h), and none of them is visible in the data file.

7. Worked example: conditioning a joint Gaussian

We close with the one conditional distribution you will use more than any other. Let x∈Rdx\in\mathbb{R}^dx∈Rd be Gaussian, p(x)=N(x∣μ,Σ)p(x) = \mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu,\Sigma)p(x)=N(x∣μ,Σ), and split it into the part you observe and the part you want:

x=(x1x2),μ=(μ1μ2),Σ=(Σ11Σ12Σ21Σ22).x = \begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mu = \begin{pmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\Sigma_{12}\\ \Sigma_{21}&\Sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}.x=(x1​x2​​),μ=(μ1​μ2​​),Σ=(Σ11​Σ21​​Σ12​Σ22​​).

Both the marginal and the conditional are again Gaussian, and (Murphy §3.2.3; we derive it by completing the square in the linear-Gaussian lecture):

p(x1)=N(x1∣μ1,Σ11), p(x1∣x2)=N(x1 ∣ μ1+Σ12Σ22−1(x2−μ2),  Σ11−Σ12Σ22−1Σ21). p(x_1) = \mathcal{N}(x_1\mid\mu_1,\Sigma_{11}), \qquad \boxed{\ p(x_1\mid x_2) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_1\ \big|\ \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2),\ \ \Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\big).\ }p(x1​)=N(x1​∣μ1​,Σ11​), p(x1​∣x2​)=N(x1​ ∣∣​ μ1​+Σ12​Σ22−1​(x2​−μ2​),  Σ11​−Σ12​Σ22−1​Σ21​). ​

Take it apart; every piece is something we built today.

  • The conditional mean is linear in the observation. By §5, E[x1∣x2]\mathbb{E}[x_1|x_2]E[x1​∣x2​] is the MSE-optimal predictor of x1x_1x1​ from x2x_2x2​ — so for jointly Gaussian variables the optimal regressor is exactly linear, with coefficients Σ12Σ22−1\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}Σ12​Σ22−1​. Linear regression is not an approximation here; it is the truth. This is the precise sense in which "Gaussian" and "linear" are one assumption seen from two sides, and it is why §3 was worth the time.
  • The conditional covariance does not depend on the observed value x2x_2x2​. You can compute how much uncertainty an experiment will remove before running it. That is what makes Bayesian experimental design and Bayesian optimization tractable.
  • Uncertainty can only shrink. Σ11−Σ12Σ22−1Σ21⪯Σ11\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\preceq\Sigma_{11}Σ11​−Σ12​Σ22−1​Σ21​⪯Σ11​ — a Schur complement — and the shrinkage is governed entirely by the cross-covariance Σ12\Sigma_{12}Σ12​. If Σ12=0\Sigma_{12}=0Σ12​=0, uncorrelated and therefore (Gaussian!) independent, the conditional equals the marginal and the observation taught you nothing.

Scalar case, worth writing out. With ρ=corr(x1,x2)\rho = \mathrm{corr}(x_1,x_2)ρ=corr(x1​,x2​):

E[x1∣x2]=μ1+ρ σ1σ2(x2−μ2),V[x1∣x2]=σ12 (1−ρ2).\mathbb{E}[x_1\mid x_2] = \mu_1 + \rho\,\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(x_2-\mu_2), \qquad \mathbb{V}[x_1\mid x_2] = \sigma_1^2\,(1-\rho^2).E[x1​∣x2​]=μ1​+ρσ2​σ1​​(x2​−μ2​),V[x1​∣x2​]=σ12​(1−ρ2).

The variance reduction is ρ2\rho^2ρ2 — the familiar "fraction of variance explained," now derived rather than asserted. At ρ=0.5\rho = 0.5ρ=0.5 you have removed only 25% of the variance.

Where this reappears. Gaussian process regression is this formula, with Σ\SigmaΣ supplied by a kernel evaluated at training and test inputs: form the joint over function values, condition on the observed ones, read off the predictive mean and variance. The Kalman filter is this formula applied recursively, alternating conditioning on a new measurement with propagation through linear dynamics via Cov[Ax]=AΣA⊤\mathrm{Cov}[Ax] = A\Sigma A^\topCov[Ax]=AΣA⊤ from §3. Bayesian linear regression is the same computation with parameters in place of function values. When we reach those topics the derivation will already be done; only the bookkeeping will be new.
