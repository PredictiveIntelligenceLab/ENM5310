1. From KL divergence to the log-likelihood

Lecture 3 posed the question what should "pθ≈qp_\theta \approx qpθ​≈q" mean? and answered it with the KL divergence. This section carries that answer all the way to a concrete optimization problem, one step at a time.

1.1 The empirical distribution, restated

Given data D={x1,…,xN}\mathcal{D} = \{x_1,\dots,x_N\}D={x1​,…,xN​}, the empirical distribution is the probability distribution placing mass 1/N1/N1/N on each observed point and no mass anywhere else:

 q(x)  ≜  1N∑n=1Nδ(x−xn). \boxed{\ q(x) \;\triangleq\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(x-x_n).\ } q(x)≜N1​n=1∑N​δ(x−xn​). ​

Here δ(⋅)\delta(\cdot)δ(⋅) is the Dirac delta, defined for our purposes entirely by how it behaves inside an integral: ∫g(x) δ(x−xn) dx=g(xn)\int g(x)\,\delta(x-x_n)\,dx = g(x_n)∫g(x)δ(x−xn​)dx=g(xn​). Everything we need about qqq follows from a single property, worth its own line because the next page uses it three times:

Eq[g(X)]  =  ∫g(x) q(x) dx  =  1N∑n=1Ng(xn).(1.1)\mathbb{E}_{q}[g(X)] \;=\; \int g(x)\,q(x)\,dx \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(x_n). \tag{1.1}Eq​[g(X)]=∫g(x)q(x)dx=N1​n=1∑N​g(xn​).(1.1)

Expectations under qqq are sample averages. That is the entire content of the empirical distribution. It is the object Lecture 1 introduced as the thing whose expectations Monte Carlo computes, and it is the only formal representation of "the data" we will need.

If XXX is discrete, qqq is an ordinary probability mass function, q(x)=1N#{n:xn=x}q(x) = \frac1N\#\{n: x_n = x\}q(x)=N1​#{n:xn​=x} — the histogram of the data, normalized to sum to one. Read δ\deltaδ as the Kronecker delta and every formula below holds verbatim.

1.2 The three information-theoretic quantities, restated

From Lecture 3, for distributions ppp and rrr on the same space:

  • Entropy: H[p]≜−Ep[log⁡p(X)]\mathbb{H}[p] \triangleq -\mathbb{E}_p[\log p(X)]H[p]≜−Ep​[logp(X)] — the average surprisal of ppp under itself.
  • Cross-entropy: H(p,r)≜−Ep[log⁡r(X)]\mathbb{H}(p,r) \triangleq -\mathbb{E}_p[\log r(X)]H(p,r)≜−Ep​[logr(X)] — the average surprisal of a model rrr when the world is actually ppp.

1.3 The derivation

We seek the model closest to the data in KL divergence:

θ^  =  arg⁡min⁡θ∈Θ KL(q ∥ pθ).\hat\theta \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta\big).θ^=argθ∈Θmin​ KL(q∥pθ​).

Step 1 — expand the definition.

KL(q∥pθ)  =  Eq ⁣[log⁡q(X)pθ(X)].\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(X)}{p_\theta(X)}\right].KL(q∥pθ​)=Eq​[logpθ​(X)q(X)​].

Step 2 — split the logarithm. Since log⁡(a/b)=log⁡a−log⁡b\log(a/b) = \log a - \log blog(a/b)=loga−logb,

KL(q∥pθ)  =  Eq[log⁡q(X)−log⁡pθ(X)].\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log q(X) - \log p_\theta(X)\big].KL(q∥pθ​)=Eq​[logq(X)−logpθ​(X)].

Step 3 — use linearity of expectation. The expectation of a difference is the difference of expectations:

KL(q∥pθ)  =  Eq[log⁡q(X)]⏟=− H[q]  −  Eq[log⁡pθ(X)].\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log q(X)]}_{\textstyle = -\,\mathbb{H}[q]} \;-\; \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_\theta(X)].KL(q∥pθ​)==−H[q]Eq​[logq(X)]​​−Eq​[logpθ​(X)].

Step 4 — discard the term free of θ\thetaθ. The first term depends only on the data. Adding a constant to an objective does not move its minimizer, so

arg⁡min⁡θ KL(q∥pθ)  =  arg⁡min⁡θ {−Eq[log⁡pθ(X)]}  =  arg⁡min⁡θ H(q,pθ).\arg\min_\theta\ \mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta) \;=\; \arg\min_\theta\ \Big\{-\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_\theta(X)]\Big\} \;=\; \arg\min_\theta\ \mathbb{H}(q,p_\theta).argθmin​ KL(q∥pθ​)=argθmin​ {−Eq​[logpθ​(X)]}=argθmin​ H(q,pθ​).

The middle expression is exactly the cross-entropy between the data and the model. Minimizing KL and minimizing cross-entropy are the same problem whenever the first argument is held fixed.

Step 5 — evaluate the expectation with property (1.1). Taking g=log⁡pθg = \log p_\thetag=logpθ​,

− Eq[log⁡pθ(X)]  =  −1N∑n=1Nlog⁡pθ(xn)  =  NLL(θ).-\,\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_\theta(X)] \;=\; -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(x_n) \;=\; \mathrm{NLL}(\theta).−Eq​[logpθ​(X)]=−N1​n=1∑N​logpθ​(xn​)=NLL(θ).

This is the step where an abstract divergence between distributions becomes a finite sum over data points. Nothing was approximated: the empirical distribution was constructed so that this expectation is exactly a sample average.

Step 6 — turn the minimization into a maximization. Multiplying by the positive constant NNN and negating converts arg⁡min⁡\arg\minargmin to arg⁡max⁡\arg\maxargmax:

arg⁡min⁡θ {−1N∑nlog⁡pθ(xn)}  =  arg⁡max⁡θ ∑n=1Nlog⁡pθ(xn).\arg\min_\theta\ \left\{-\frac1N\sum_n \log p_\theta(x_n)\right\} \;=\; \arg\max_\theta\ \sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(x_n).argθmin​ {−N1​n∑​logpθ​(xn​)}=argθmax​ n=1∑N​logpθ​(xn​).

Step 7 — recognize the result. Because the data are i.i.d., the joint density factorizes, pθ(D)=∏npθ(xn)p_\theta(\mathcal{D}) = \prod_n p_\theta(x_n)pθ​(D)=∏n​pθ​(xn​), so ∑nlog⁡pθ(xn)=log⁡pθ(D)\sum_n \log p_\theta(x_n) = \log p_\theta(\mathcal{D})∑n​logpθ​(xn​)=logpθ​(D); and taking a logarithm does not move an argmax, since log⁡\loglog is strictly increasing. Assembling everything:

 arg⁡min⁡θKL(q∥pθ)  =  arg⁡min⁡θH(q,pθ)  =  arg⁡max⁡θ∑n=1Nlog⁡pθ(xn)  =  arg⁡max⁡θ pθ(D)  =  θ^MLE. \boxed{\ \arg\min_\theta \mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta) \;=\; \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{H}(q,p_\theta) \;=\; \arg\max_\theta \sum_{n=1}^N \log p_\theta(x_n) \;=\; \arg\max_\theta\, p_\theta(\mathcal{D}) \;=\; \hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}.\ } argθmin​KL(q∥pθ​)=argθmin​H(q,pθ​)=argθmax​n=1∑N​logpθ​(xn​)=argθmax​pθ​(D)=θ^MLE​. ​

Maximum likelihood is not a separate principle that happens to work well. It is what "make the model close to the data" evaluates to when closeness is measured by KL divergence and the data is represented by its empirical distribution.

Two consequences worth carrying forward:

  • The cross-entropy loss of classification is literally H(q,pθ)\mathbb{H}(q,p_\theta)H(q,pθ​). The name is not an analogy.
  • Squared error is a cross-entropy in disguise: under a Gaussian likelihood the negative log-likelihood is a scaled sum of squared residuals plus a constant, as §6 makes explicit.

1.4 A technical caveat, stated honestly

When XXX is continuous, qqq places positive probability on finitely many points and therefore has no density with respect to Lebesgue measure. In that case H[q]\mathbb{H}[q]H[q] and KL(q∥pθ)\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta)KL(q∥pθ​) are not finite numbers, and Steps 1–3 are formal manipulations rather than statements about real quantities.

Everything from Step 4 onward survives intact. The cross-entropy H(q,pθ)=−1N∑nlog⁡pθ(xn)\mathbb{H}(q,p_\theta) = -\frac1N\sum_n\log p_\theta(x_n)H(q,pθ​)=−N1​∑n​logpθ​(xn​) is a perfectly well-defined finite number for any density pθp_\thetapθ​, and it is the only term carrying θ\thetaθ. So the precise version of the boxed statement is:

Maximum likelihood minimizes the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution and the model. For discrete XXX it additionally minimizes KL(q∥pθ)\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta)KL(q∥pθ​) exactly. For continuous XXX, the corresponding KL statement is a statement about the N→∞N\to\inftyN→∞ limit, which §2 makes rigorous.

Use "MLE minimizes KL(q∥pθ)\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta)KL(q∥pθ​)" as a mnemonic, since the intuition it carries is correct. Do not mistake it for a theorem in the continuous case.

2. What maximum likelihood converges to

Section 1 characterized the finite-NNN objective. This section characterizes what its minimizer approaches as data accumulate, and the answer is more interesting than "the truth."

By the law of large numbers (Lecture 1), for each fixed θ\thetaθ the sample average converges to the population expectation:

NLL(θ)=−1N∑n=1Nlog⁡pθ(xn)  ⟶  −Ep⋆[log⁡pθ(X)]  =  H(p⋆,pθ)  =  KL(p⋆∥pθ)+H[p⋆].\mathrm{NLL}(\theta) = -\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N \log p_\theta(x_n) \;\longrightarrow\; -\mathbb{E}_{p^\star}\big[\log p_\theta(X)\big] \;=\; \mathbb{H}(p^\star,p_\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{KL}(p^\star\|p_\theta) + \mathbb{H}[p^\star].NLL(θ)=−N1​n=1∑N​logpθ​(xn​)⟶−Ep⋆​[logpθ​(X)]=H(p⋆,pθ​)=KL(p⋆∥pθ​)+H[p⋆].

The final term does not involve θ\thetaθ. Under conditions making this convergence uniform over Θ\ThetaΘ — enough that the location of the minimum converges and not merely its value — the minimizers converge as well:

 θ^MLE  ⟶  θ⋆  ≜  arg⁡min⁡θ∈Θ KL(p⋆ ∥ pθ). \boxed{\ \hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}} \;\longrightarrow\; \theta^\star \;\triangleq\; \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \mathrm{KL}\big(p^\star\,\|\,p_\theta\big).\ } θ^MLE​⟶θ⋆≜argθ∈Θmin​ KL(p⋆∥pθ​). ​

Note the structure. At finite NNN we minimize a divergence to the empirical distribution; in the limit, a divergence to the true distribution. The gap between these two targets is where overfitting lives, and it is the same gap Lecture 1 identified in distinguishing the empirical distribution from the one that generated it.

The well-specified case. If the truth lies in the family, p⋆=pθ0p^\star = p_{\theta_0}p⋆=pθ0​​ for some θ0∈Θ\theta_0\in\Thetaθ0​∈Θ, then KL(p⋆∥pθ0)=0\mathrm{KL}(p^\star\|p_{\theta_0}) = 0KL(p⋆∥pθ0​​)=0 attains the minimum, so θ⋆=θ0\theta^\star = \theta_0θ⋆=θ0​: the MLE recovers the truth. This is the case classical theory analyzes.

The misspecified case. If p⋆∉Pp^\star\notin\mathcal{P}p⋆∈/P — the normal situation for any model of a real system — the MLE still converges, but to the projection of p⋆p^\starp⋆ onto P\mathcal{P}P in the sense of forward KL. That projection has a specific and predictable character. The divergence

KL(p⋆∥pθ)=∫p⋆(x) log⁡p⋆(x)pθ(x) dx\mathrm{KL}(p^\star\|p_\theta) = \int p^\star(x)\,\log\frac{p^\star(x)}{p_\theta(x)}\,dxKL(p⋆∥pθ​)=∫p⋆(x)logpθ​(x)p⋆(x)​dx

is an average weighted by p⋆p^\starp⋆. Wherever p⋆p^\starp⋆ has mass and pθp_\thetapθ​ has none, the integrand is +∞+\infty+∞; wherever pθp_\thetapθ​ has mass and p⋆p^\starp⋆ has none, the integrand contributes nothing at all. The asymmetry is total: the fitted model is punished for missing regions where data occurred, and not punished for inventing regions where none did.

The consequence is mass covering. Fit a single Gaussian by maximum likelihood to bimodal data and the result is a wide Gaussian straddling both modes, placing most of its probability in the valley between them where nothing was ever observed. This is not a failure of the optimizer; it is the exact minimizer of the divergence that was chosen.

Lecture 3 §8 distinguished the two directions of KL. Variational inference, later in the course, minimizes the reverse divergence KL(pθ∥p⋆)\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\|p^\star)KL(pθ​∥p⋆), whose characteristic failure is the opposite one — it locks onto a single mode and reports too little uncertainty. The theme is worth carrying: every approximate inference method in this course is characterized by which direction of KL it minimizes, and therefore by the specific way in which it misleads you.

3. The estimator is a random variable

This section makes the conceptual move on which the rest of the lecture depends.

The estimator θ^=θ^(D)\hat\theta = \hat\theta(\mathcal{D})θ^=θ^(D) is a function of the data. The data are random. A function of a random variable is a random variable. Therefore θ^\hat\thetaθ^ has a distribution.

Sampling distribution. For an estimator θ^(D)\hat\theta(\mathcal{D})θ^(D) computed from data D\mathcal{D}D drawn from p⋆p^\starp⋆, the sampling distribution of θ^\hat\thetaθ^ is the distribution of θ^(D)\hat\theta(\mathcal{D})θ^(D) induced by the randomness of D\mathcal{D}D. It describes how the estimate would vary across hypothetical repetitions of the entire experiment.

You have one draw from this distribution. Every statement you can make about the reliability of a fit is a statement about a distribution you have sampled exactly once. This is why the closed-form expressions in §4.1 and §6.1 are valuable: they describe a distribution you cannot otherwise observe.

Let θ⋆\theta^\starθ⋆ be the target from §2. Define

3.2 Consistency

Convergence in probability. θ^N→pθ⋆\hat\theta_N\xrightarrow{p}\theta^\starθ^N​p​θ⋆ means: for every ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, Pr⁡[∥θ^N−θ⋆∥>ε]→0\Pr\big[\|\hat\theta_N-\theta^\star\|>\varepsilon\big]\to0Pr[∥θ^N​−θ⋆∥>ε]→0 as N→∞N\to\inftyN→∞.

7. When the MLE fails: three degeneracies, one cause

Everything so far has assumed the maximizer exists and is unique. Frequently it does not.

(a) The collapsing variance. With N=1N=1N=1, the Gaussian MLE gives μ^=x1\hat\mu = x_1μ^​=x1​ and σ^2=0\hat\sigma^2 = 0σ^2=0, and the likelihood is unbounded: as σ→0\sigma\to0σ→0 with μ=x1\mu = x_1μ=x1​, the density at x1x_1x1​ diverges. The same pathology appears at scale in mixture models, where the likelihood is infinite whenever a component collapses onto a single data point. Any EM implementation written without a variance floor will eventually find it.

(b) The underdetermined regression. If D>ND>ND>N, or if two feature columns are linearly dependent, Φ⊤Φ\Phi^\top\PhiΦ⊤Φ is singular. An entire affine subspace of www then achieves the same maximum likelihood, and w^\hat ww^ is not well defined. Equivalently, by §6.1, the sampling variance along those directions is infinite.

(c) Separability. For logistic regression on linearly separable data — data that some hyperplane divides perfectly — replacing www by αw\alpha wαw and letting α→∞\alpha\to\inftyα→∞ drives every predicted probability to 000 or 111 and pushes the likelihood monotonically toward a supremum that is never attained. The MLE does not exist: ∥w^∥=∞\|\hat w\| = \infty∥w^∥=∞. You will watch an optimizer march off toward infinity in the next lecture, and should recognize it as this rather than as a bug.

The common cause. In every case the log-likelihood is flat, or unbounded, along some direction of parameter space, because the data carries no information about that direction. The likelihood is a function of the data alone. Where the data is silent, the likelihood is silent, and the maximizer is undefined or meaningless.

It follows that any repair must contribute information that is not a function of the data. That is exactly what a prior is.

8. MAP: the prior as curvature

Bayes' rule from Lecture 2 gives the posterior p(θ∣D)∝p(D∣θ) p(θ)p(\theta\mid\mathcal{D})\propto p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)p(θ∣D)∝p(D∣θ)p(θ). The maximum a posteriori estimate is its mode:

10. Two things the MAP estimate is not

These two facts are the reason the course does not stop at MAP.

10.1 MAP is not invariant to reparameterization

The MLE is. If η=g(θ)\eta = g(\theta)η=g(θ) for an invertible, differentiable ggg, then

η^MLE=g(θ^MLE),\hat\eta_{\mathrm{MLE}} = g\big(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}\big),η^​MLE​=g(θ^MLE​),

because the likelihood is a function of θ\thetaθ, not a density in θ\thetaθ: its value at a point is unchanged by relabeling the points, so the location of the maximum simply transports.

A prior, however, is a density in θ\thetaθ. By the change-of-variables formula from Lecture 3, the induced prior on η\etaη is

pη(η)=pθ(g−1(η)) ∣det⁡∂g−1∂η∣,p_\eta(\eta) = p_\theta\big(g^{-1}(\eta)\big)\,\left|\det\frac{\partial g^{-1}}{\partial\eta}\right| ,pη​(η)=pθ​(g−1(η))∣∣​det∂η∂g−1​∣∣​,

and the Jacobian factor varies with η\etaη, so it moves the location of the maximum. Concretely: fit a scale parameter by MAP in the coordinate σ\sigmaσ, then fit it by MAP in the coordinate log⁡σ\log\sigmalogσ under the correspondingly transformed prior, and after mapping one answer back through ggg the two will disagree. The MAP estimate depends on the coordinate system in which the model happened to be written.

Every other summary of the posterior — the mean, the quantiles, the predictive distribution, any posterior expectation — transforms correctly, because integration against a density is invariant while maximization of a density is not. The mode is the odd one out.

10.2 The mode is not where the mass is

Consider θ∼N(0,Id)\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)θ∼N(0,Id​) in ddd dimensions. The density is maximized at the origin. Now ask where samples actually fall, by studying ∥θ∥2=∑j=1dθj2\|\theta\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_j^2∥θ∥2=∑j=1d​θj2​:

Typical set (informal). The region containing essentially all of a distribution's probability mass. For a high-dimensional Gaussian it is a thin shell, not a ball around the mode.

The ratio of the density on that shell to the density at the mode is e−d/2e^{-d/2}e−d/2 — astronomically small — and yet the shell is where all the samples are, because the volume available grows faster than the density decays. In d=1000d=1000d=1000, essentially no sample from the distribution lands anywhere near its own mode.

The consequence: a MAP estimate can be an atypical, unrepresentative point of the very distribution it summarizes, and substituting it into a prediction discards all uncertainty in the process. This is not a small correction to be patched later. It is the reason the remaining Bayesian material in this course exists.

11. Where this leaves us

We now have a principle (maximum likelihood, derived from KL), a characterization of what it converges to (§2), a description of how reliable its answers are (§3, §4.1, §6.1), an account of when it breaks (§7), and a repair (§8). Two things are missing.

Computation. Both closed-form solutions came from setting a gradient to zero and being fortunate enough to solve the resulting equation. Logistic regression — a model already written down in Lecture 3 — admits no such solution. The next lecture derives its gradient and Hessian, then takes up the general question of what to do when ∇ℓ(θ)=0\nabla\ell(\theta)=0∇ℓ(θ)=0 cannot be solved in closed form. That is the entry point to every optimizer used in the second half of the course.

Uncertainty. Section 10 argues that a point estimate is a poor summary of what the data supports. Lecture 6 keeps the entire posterior instead.
